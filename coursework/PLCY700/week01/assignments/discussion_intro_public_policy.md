## Part 2

It's my observation that the author was successful in identifying key principles and concepts from Stone's framework which helped the APR succeed in their initiative – the preservation of the American prairie. Nonetheless, more importantly it is my opinion that Angstadt (2020), was most successful in helping to shed light of some of the critical limitations and gaps which Stone's framework fails to capture. In his analysis, Angstadt (2020) effectively demonstrated how using a modified approach – that which strategically omits the moral dimension in traditional causal stories, indeed leads to a more favorable outcome. In this case, this helps to explain the success the APR had in its mission and objectives.

The APR's journey and approach towards the conservation and restoration of the prairie ecosystem is an interesting study. This group began to see positive results as they applied some conventional causal story principles which were discussed by Stone (1989). However, the group also modified their approach as they recognized that not all aspects of Stone's framework are universally applicable to every case. Stone's framework presents the fact that policy problems are defined through both empirical and moral dimensions. The latter one more often involving and attributing blame as a means to push an agenda forward. We see this approach presented all around us in news articles and in our social channels all the time. Nonetheless, in this case, the APR applied only an asymmetrical framework that embraced the empirical reality while excluding the moral dimension of blame. I believe that the APR recognized that if they were to assign blame it would not foster the needed and essential cooperation needed from the RSA to advance and promote their cause. There are a couple of approaches I would like to highlight.

First, Angstadt (2020) found merit in several aspects of Stone's framework – though not all. His research indicates that when parties benefit from the status quo, they often actively resist new causal narratives that challenge their position. In our case we see this from both the ranchers and conservationists. Angstadt observed that ranchers tried to resist the change by denying the existence of a problem at all with the prairie lands. He further supported this by citing several statements from the Ranchers Stewardship Alliance (RSA) website. Most of these statements attributed most land degradation to earlier periods like the Dust Bowl era (Angstadt, 2020, p. 8). It is interesting to see that the RSA also claimed to "now engage directly with professional conservationists to identify ways that conservation and ranching can provide mutual support" for the land (Angstadt, 2020, p. 8). This highlights their strategic framing to preserve their position regarding the prairie lands.

To me, another powerful illustration of Stone's framework in Angstadt's work is his robust application of the idea that policy problems are not objective facts but are strategically "defined" by political actors to serve their interests. Stone (1989) eloquently stated that "problem definition is a process of image making…" (p. 4), where individuals and groups craft narratives to garner support for their proposals. Angstadt (2020) grounded much of his study on this premise, while meticulously analyzing how both the RSA and the APR utilized narratives to frame the complex issues of land management and species preservation in Montana. This was exemplified as the RSA made the decision to "address regional landscape change by avoiding human-oriented problem definitions in its official statements" (Angstadt, 2020, p. 8). This is a textbook example to me on how the RSA strategically shaped its narrative and its image.

Second, and perhaps more profoundly, Angstadt (2020) recognized a fundamental limitation in Stone's framework. Angstadt points out that while Stone's framework is powerful and insightful, it often operates on the premise of a dichotomous and conflictual situation. In my view, his primary contribution is the compelling idea that an actor can deliberately choose to exclude the moral dimension – the assignment of blame and responsibility for outcomes – from a causal story. By making this choice, the actor acknowledges that such an adversarial stance would be detrimental, especially when collaboration and assistance from the opposing party is essential. This reminds me of John Nash's economic concept of the Nash equilibrium, where no party can independently improve their outcome without the mutual consideration or strategic cooperation of each other.

An example in the study of this principle is highlighted when the American Prairie Reserve (APR) avoided clear causal arguments about regional bison decline, simply stating they "reintroduced plains bison to the prairie of northeastern Montana after an absence of 120 years" (Angstadt, 2020, p. 9). The APR's focus is consistently on future restoration and collaboration. Again, this is exemplified by their statement: "we believe a prairie reserve and agricultural enterprise can thrive together" (Angstadt, 2020, p. 9). These examples illustrate how both groups strategically frame issues to maintain a non-adversarial stance.

Angstadt (2020) further articulated that in scenarios marked by significant complexity and as multiple parties may be involved, an adversarial model of a party's causal story may not always present the most effective results. This is true as we can see in the APR's situation: the very parties they might traditionally blame are also the same ones whose cooperation they need to acquire land and to achieve their objectives. As Angstadt (2020) noted, "the same individual that conservation groups like the APR might otherwise be inclined to blame for displacing native species… the APR's efforts would be hindered by adversarial causal framing" (p. 2). Thus, the intricate web of relationships between the parties involved clearly demonstrates the challenges of applying Stone's exact framework without any modifications. The intricacies of the "American Serengeti" case also highlight a key challenge: it's often difficult to establish clear moral or even empirical causes for complex problems. One must be very careful about analyzing and studying out the issue or entire set of issues and all parties potentially involved – including their motives and reasons. This makes me believe that it is easier for those parties given the status quo to claim success, especially when any type of solid evidence is difficult to obtain or put together. This scenario is not unique; Thomas S. Birkland (2016) points out how the DARE program in schools, despite studies showing its limited effectiveness – persists because the program involves trusted figures like police officers (Birkland, 2016, p. 20). Similarly, Stone (1989) reminded us that "The political success of causal theories is also constrained by two powerful social institutions for determining cause and legitimating claims about harms: law and science" (p. 294). This underscores Angstadt's (2020) observation that entrenched groups, like the ranchers, can maintain their practices and positive image when strong opposing empirical or moral arguments are challenging to establish especially when negativity is avoided (p. 8).

In conclusion, while Angstadt's (2020) application of Stone's (1989) "causal stories" framework successfully highlighted how political actors strategically define problems and how parties of the status quo resist new theories, in my opinion his most significant contribution is the identifying of a crucial limitation of the framework. Angstadt effectively demonstrated that in complex, multi-stakeholder scenarios, the strategic omission of the moral dimension (blame) from a causal story can be more effective for achieving collaborative outcomes, as exemplified by the APR's approach.
