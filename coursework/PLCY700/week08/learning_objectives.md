# Week 8 — Learning Objectives & Study Guide

## Overview (Instructor's Module Introduction, Verbatim)
In this final course module, you will begin to evaluate various theories related to public policy. You will move beyond simple definitions as you consider underlying assumptions of these theories and how they apply to specific policy situations.

## Learning Outcomes (Instructor's Official List)
Upon successful completion of this module, you will be able to:
1. Evaluate various theories related to public policy.
2. Assess the underlying assumptions of public policy theories.
3. Apply specific theories to a policy issue.

**Core Learning Activity:** Post a discussion thread (1,000–1,250 words) addressing theoretical pluralism in the policy process, applying Birkland/Stone frameworks to rights and power, and comparing empirical vs. anecdotal evidence — then post two peer replies (400–500 words each).

## Required Readings for This Module
- **Birkland, T. A.** — *An Introduction to the Policy Process*, Chapters 10–11
- **Stone, D.** — *Policy Paradox: The Art of Political Decision Making*, Chapters 12–16

## Discussion Thread: Science and Theory — Official Instructions (Verbatim)

**Part 1:** Many scholars mix and match elements of the theories presented in Birkland, chapter 11 to explain how specific policies are made, or to develop theory about a particular kind of policy making. Is it sensible to borrow from more than one theory of the policy process when testing theory? What does it say about theory that it is possible to borrow from different models? Of the five models presented in the chapter, are there certain combinations that would be totally incompatible? Why or why not, and why does this matter to the study of the policy process?

**Part 2:** Which theories outlined in your Birkland text might be best used to explain the policy questions related to rights or power outlined in Stone, chapters 15 and 16?

**Part 3:** Consider the difference between empirical scientific evidence and anecdotal evidence. Which is considered more useful to the study of public policy and why?

## Submitted Thread — Topic & Argument Summary
**Status: Complete.** Posted 2026-10-15. Full submission: [assignments/science_and_theory_homework.md](assignments/science_and_theory_homework.md) (~1,162 words).

- **Part 1 (Theoretical pluralism):** Argues that Birkland's five policy process frameworks (Stages Heuristic, ACF, PET, MSF, IAD) are complementary partial tools rather than a single macro model, since political science lacks a unified econometric framework. Flags the IAD vs. MSF combination as the clearest incompatibility — IAD's rational-choice, structured-action-arena assumptions directly contradict MSF's "garbage can" model of ambiguous, serendipitous coupling; combining them naively produces an untestable argument.
- **Part 2 (Rights & Power):** Maps Stone's Ch. 15 (Rights) onto the Advocacy Coalition Framework — rights as tools of collective mobilization that coalitions use for legal venue-shopping around deep core/policy core beliefs. Maps Stone's Ch. 16 (Power) onto Schneider & Ingram's Social Construction of Target Populations — power as the ability to structure perceptions and categorize citizens as advantaged/contenders/dependents/deviants, encoding power asymmetries into law before overt conflict occurs.
- **Part 3 (Empirical vs. anecdotal evidence):** Argues the two serve different, complementary purposes rather than competing — empirical evidence is indispensable for technical feasibility and causal rigor, while anecdotal evidence does work empirical data cannot: agenda-setting/problem definition (focusing events), causal attribution/blame (Stone's causal stories), and surfacing street-level implementation realities that dashboards miss.
