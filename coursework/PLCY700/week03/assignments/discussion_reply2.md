Peter, thank you for sharing your analysis of Early's (2023) academic book review of Matthew Desmond's work. Your evaluation indeed highlighted the several critical observations which call out the danger of proposing public policy solutions without having a solid foundation of empirical evidence and a testable hypothesis.

I completely agree with your observations regarding the author's failure to formulate an actual hypothesis to drive the narrative. As we have seen these past weeks in our course readings, scientific inquiry relies on the ability to test and potentially disprove claims. Karl Popper emphasized that for a theory to be academically valid, it must be strictly falsifiable (Walliman, 2011). Instead of utilizing a central hypothesis to guide the research and to establish his measurable variables, it appears Desmond merely presented a narrative of identified problems and proposed solutions. Without this testable hypothesis, his proposed solutions cannot be empirically verified. This leaves these as subjective recommendations rather than scientifically grounded policy interventions.

Furthermore, your point that the reviewer found empirical evidence lacking in Desmond's approach is a crucial observation. Indeed, empirical data is the cornerstone of academic credibility. In Chapter 3 of *Your Research Project*, Walliman (2011) stresses the importance of providing transparent context and meaningful background information regarding research methodologies. When authors fail to provide this methodological context – or intentionally omit significant counter-studies, as it was noted by Early, it becomes extremely difficult for reviewers and policymakers to understand the relevancy and validity of the presented facts. If the data is cherry-picked rather than systematically gathered, the research loses its objective authority and functions more like political rhetoric.

Regarding the importance of data, I made one additional observation regarding the latter part of your post. The reviewer noted that the issue of poverty has grown exponentially since the onset of COVID-19 and that prices of necessities have increased. While this is a widely accepted social observation, applying the same critical lens we used on Desmond, it would have been highly valuable to include specific empirical metrics or statistical sources to ground that claim. For instance, referencing specific historical macroeconomic data or poverty indicators would provide the exact evidence needed to validate the severity of the financial crisis the reviewer described. As Stone (2020) argues in her work on metrics, the way we formally quantify a problem directly dictates how it is prioritized, viewed, and used in the political agenda.
