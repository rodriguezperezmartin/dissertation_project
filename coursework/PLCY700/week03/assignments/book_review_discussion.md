## Part I: Evaluation of a Peer-Reviewed Book Review

To gain an understanding of the structural and analytical requirements of academic critiques, I chose to evaluate Laura Marotta's (2021) peer-reviewed evaluation of Deborah Stone's (2020) book, *Counting: How We Use Numbers to Decide What Matters*. It was published in the *Canadian Journal of Educational Administration and Policy*. In this peer review, Marotta structures her critique firstly by introducing Stone's main thesis: policymakers and society must fundamentally revise how statistics are generated to avoid perpetuating societal inequalities. Marotta highlights Stone's foundational argument that numbers are not merely objective facts, but rather powerful weapons that can be wielded for both positive progress and systemic oppression.

Following this contextual introduction from Marotta (2021), she transitions into the objective summary of the book's historical and contemporary examples. In this section, she outlines Stone's assertion that many widely accepted metrics are indeed inherently biased or that these rely on erroneous computations. These inaccurate numbers include macroeconomic indicators like Gross Domestic Product (GDP), predictive justice algorithms like COMPAS, unemployment statistics, standardized testing, and even the definitions created by institutions such as poverty. Marotta shares how these numbers are weaponized by politicians and people in power, while also balancing the narrative by showing how diligent researchers use numbers to expose injustices, such as the Flint, Michigan water crisis (Marotta, 2021, p. 124). Furthermore, Marotta also outlines Stone's research methodology, noting that the author synthesized evidence from peer-reviewed journals, newspaper articles, books, conference reports, and social media channels to construct and put together her qualitative observations (Marotta, 2021, p. 124).

The pivot point of the review occurs when Marotta (2021) shifts her presentation from the objective summary to critical analysis. While Marotta praises Stone's "tongue-in-cheek humor" and her effective way of exposing various "counting offenders," she does push back heavily against some of the author's blind spots. For instance, Marotta flags a significant contradiction in Stone's argument regarding the judicial system. Stone heavily relies on judges to uphold fairness and the will of the people; however, Marotta critically and rightfully observes that Stone fails to recognize that these judicial individuals are inherently political and subject to their own biased views based on biased and partial statistics (Marotta, 2021, p. 125).

More importantly, Marotta (2021) cautions against Stone's extreme cynicism toward quantitative metrics in our society. Stone's position is so strong that it borders on suggesting that our society might be better off by discarding certain metrics. Marotta counters this by arguing that we must not forget the power that data possesses to illuminate marginalized populations. To prove this and to emphasize this argument, Marotta references the recent tragic discovery of 215 Indigenous children that were found in a mass grave at a former Canadian residential school. For decades there were stories of missing Indigenous children, but it was not until the concrete number "215" was brought forth following the publicized discovery of the mass grave that the issue gained traction. This number, now a symbol, has helped to progress the country's moral policy efforts forward (Marotta, 2021, p. 125).

Overall, this evaluation process has provided me with a clear blueprint for the format and the process an academic book review follows (San Jose State University Writing Center, n.d.). Successful reviewers must quickly introduce the subject, they must provide contextual background, and explain the specific literature gap the book attempts to fill. The reviewer must then present a neutral, objective summary of the author's main arguments – assertions, chapter progression, and supporting evidence. Finally, the reviewer must execute a critical pivot in which they offer their own analytical insights regarding the author's methodological strengths and weaknesses before delivering a final recommendation and conclusion. For my own book review assignment in Module 6, I will ensure I do not merely summarize the text, but that I do an effective job to actively interrogate its assumptions and biases just as Marotta did.

## Part II: Module 6 Book Selection

For my Book Review Assignment in Module 6, I have chosen Carol H. Weiss's (1991) *Organizations for Policy Analysis: Helping Government Think*. I selected this book because it aligns perfectly with some of the work I am doing for my dissertation regarding macroeconomic public policy and the implementation of digital healthcare infrastructures. Specifically, Weiss's work provides theoretical insights into how fragmented government agencies process information and how they integrate data-driven insight platforms. As emerging economies attempt to modernize their own healthcare systems, there is a crucial need to transition away from disjointed, legacy Electronic Health Record (EHR) systems or no systems at all and move toward a unified clinical decision support process and digital pipeline.

I strongly believe that to create effective public policies which will help emerging economies provide more equitable and efficient healthcare to its citizens, policy makers must have the right information on hand, functioning as "just weights" for equitable resource distribution and access. I believe Weiss's text will shed light on the systemic gaps in how government bureaucracies currently digest analytical research and data. Reading and critiquing this book will not only provide me with the opportunity to practice the academic review framework process outlined in Part 1 of this paper, but it will also provide me with foundational literature for my own dissertation regarding institutional data governance.
