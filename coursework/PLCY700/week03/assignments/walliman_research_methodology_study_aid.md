Structured overview of the core research methodology principles from Walliman, covering measurement, logical argumentation, and fallacy identification — directly supports this week's learning outcomes on levels of measurement and inductive/deductive reasoning.

## 1. Levels of Measurement
- **Ordinal:** Clear logical order/ranking, but unequal or unmeasurable distances between categories.
  - e.g., Comfort level (very uncomfortable → very comfortable); Wealth (destitute → wealthy)
- **Interval:** Clear order and equal, measurable distances between values, but no true absolute zero.
  - e.g., IQ (90, 102, 125, 130) — an IQ of 0 doesn't mean a total absence of intelligence

## 2. Statements vs. Arguments
- **Associational (Relational) Statements:** Descriptive connections between variables (positive/negative correlation); no premises or conclusions.
  - Negative correlation e.g.: as tax on petrol increases, traffic decreases; as marrying couple's age increases, wedding size decreases
- **Arguments:** One or more premises logically support a conclusion (explicit or implicit).

## 3. Deductive vs. Inductive Arguments
- **Deductive:** Conclusion follows with absolute logical certainty from the premises.
  - e.g., Car was $5,000, paid $500 deposit → owe $4,500
  - Quiz note: "There are traces of non-permitted chemicals in his blood; he has obviously been taking stimulants" was classified as **deductive**.
- **Inductive:** Conclusion is probable, not guaranteed, from specific observations.
  - e.g., A boy played professional soccer at 16; he must have been a good junior player.
  - e.g., The Minister looked pale after a meeting; it looks as if they gave him an ultimatum.

## 4. Logical Consistency
- **Consistent belief systems:** Claims don't contradict each other and can logically coexist.
  - e.g., Peter is the most generous member; he paid for the team's travel expenses.
  - e.g., Killing animals is immoral; therefore, live animals should not be exported for slaughter.
- **Inconsistent belief systems:** Claims contradict each other or can't reasonably be true simultaneously.
  - e.g., Claiming to have never sat on a horse, but also claiming the ability to win a show jumping competition in two minutes.
  - e.g., Knowing you will never get ill, but admitting you just caught a disease.

## 5. Logical Fallacies

### Informal Fallacies (flaws in content/meaning/assumptions)
- **False Analogy (Weak Analogy):** Comparing things alike superficially but fundamentally different (e.g., running a complex government vs. steering a ship).
- **Appeal to Ignorance:** Claim is true because not proven false, or false because not proven true (e.g., "The Loch Ness monster doesn't exist because every attempt to find it has failed").

### Formal Fallacies (flaws in structural logic, regardless of content)
- **Affirming the Consequent:** "If P, then Q. Q is true. Therefore, P is true." (e.g., "If Americans want good trade, they encourage specialist goods. They encourage specialist goods, therefore they want good trade relations.")
