## 1. Common Themes, Assumptions, or Approaches

**Theme A — Technology's potential consistently outpaces its real-world adoption.** Across the technology-focused sources, the framing is nearly identical: a valuable capability exists, but organizations are slow or inconsistent in actually using it. Wu et al. frame Healthcare Technology Management (HTM) competency as underdeveloped relative to its impact on IT-healthcare partnerships; Alkhwaldi notes business intelligence (BI) has "grown in popularity" but suffers from a "lack of exploration" in healthcare specifically; the Sweden AI study explicitly cites AI's "slow and variable uptake"; Alraja frames IoT adoption as under-analyzed; and Beaulie states the healthcare supply chain "lags far behind" other industries in digitalization.

> **Supporting evidence:**
> 1. "Due to its ability to support well-informed decision-making, business intelligence (BI) has grown in popularity among executives across a range of industries. However, given the volume of data collected in health-care organizations, there is a lack of exploration concerning its implementation." (Alkhwaldi, 2023, p. 2135)
> 2. "The healthcare supply chain lags far behind supply chains in other industries in terms of performance and the deployment of best practices." (Beaulieu & Bentahar, 2021, p. 1)
> 3. "[N]ovel technologies are often resisted by healthcare leaders, which contributes to their slow and variable uptake." (Petersson et al., 2022, p. 1)
> 4. "Aiming to address a distinct gap in the literature, this work assessed gender and generational differences in the factors influencing FHPs['] BI towards IoT enabled HAs..." (Alraja, 2022, p. 9)
> 5. "As a part of the changing world, the healthcare industry and healthcare system have encountered dramatic changes in information and communication technology (ICT) that will influence the way healthcare services are delivered and used..." (Wu, Chen, & Greenes, 2009, p. 72)

**Theme B — Adoption is gated by human and organizational trust/readiness, not technical capability.** Wu, Alkhwaldi, Alraja, and the Sweden AI study each isolate "soft" variables — trust, perceived risk, professional autonomy, medical-legal risk, decision-making culture, change-management capacity — as the actual predictors of adoption, more so than the technology's technical performance. This is a consistent assumption underlying the quantitative acceptance-modeling literature (UTAUT, Theory of Planned Behavior, Privacy Calculus Theory).

> **Supporting evidence:**
> 1. "Medical doctors and practitioners in health-care organizations may perceive BI systems as an imminent threat to their professional autonomy as offering prescription advice for care planning and giving recommendations during counselling sessions with patients can be perceived as intrusions on their professional autonomy." (Alkhwaldi, 2023, p. 2145)
> 2. "[I]t is important that healthcare professionals have trust in recommendations from the AI systems in clinical practice. If some healthcare professionals do not trust the AI systems and their output, there is a risk that they will not use them in clinical practice." (Petersson et al., 2022, p. 10)
> 3. "[M]uch of the current academic focus has been on issues surrounding trust — how best to ensure data security and legislative compliance while still reliably providing all users with an appropriate level of detail." (Alraja, 2022, p. 3)
> 4. "[T]he cross-sectional design of this research makes it more difficult to prove causality... Experiments or longitudinal studies may offer more reliable insights into the dynamics of" acceptance over time. (Alkhwaldi, 2023, p. 2154) — underscores that acceptance/trust is treated as a dynamic, not fixed, gatekeeping variable.

**Theme C — Structural and social barriers drive healthcare inequity independent of technology.** Handtke (cultural competence), Coombs (rural access), Lebano (migrants/refugees in Europe), and the health migrations study (transnational healthcare practices) converge on a different axis entirely: access problems rooted in language, culture, geography, legal status, and cost — not information or technology gaps.

> **Supporting evidence:**
> 1. "[A] friction exists between aspects of patients' rural identities and healthcare systems." (Coombs et al., 2022, p. 6)
> 2. "[D]espite the aspiration to ensure equality of access to healthcare, there is evidence of persistent inequalities between migrants and non-migrants in access to healthcare services... The evidence shows unmet healthcare needs, especially when it comes to mental and dental health as well as the existence of legal barriers in accessing healthcare." (Lebano et al., 2020, p. 1)
> 3. "The most common structural challenges migrants encountered in the US healthcare system were: 1) documentation status; 2) US healthcare system complexity; and 3) affordability." (Joseph, health migrations study, p. 5 [PDF pagination — no fixed print page numbers available for this source])
> 4. "Culturally and linguistically diverse patients access healthcare services less than the host populations and are confronted with different barriers such as language barriers, legal restrictions or differences in health beliefs." (Handtke, Schilgen, & Mösko, 2019, p. 1 [PDF pagination — PLOS ONE does not use fixed print page numbers])

**Theme D — Acceptance/acceptability itself is treated as the dependent variable determining success.** Sharp's public health policy acceptability survey and the Sweden AI leadership study both frame the research question not as "does this work?" but "will people accept it?" — a shared assumption that acceptance is the binding constraint on policy or technology success.

> **Supporting evidence:**
> 1. "For public health policies to be effective, it is critical that they are acceptable to the public as acceptance levels impact success rate." (Sharp et al., 2020, p. 802)
> 2. "[H]ealth policy discussions can often occur in the absence of knowledge on public opinions on such policies..." (Sharp et al., 2020, p. 803)
> 3. "Findings show an appetite for public health policies among the majority of the public. The relationship between supporting policies and engaging in healthy behaviours suggests a feedback loop that is potentially capable of shifting both public opinion and the opportunities for policy intervention." (Sharp et al., 2020, p. 802)
> 4. "[H]ealthcare leaders are expected to have a key role in the implementation and use of AI for the development of healthcare... It is essential to understand the perspectives of healthcare leaders, because they have a key role in the implementation process of new technologies in healthcare." (Petersson et al., 2022, pp. 1, 3)

## 2. Common Weaknesses or Limitations

1. **Cross-sectional design dominates the quantitative studies** (Alkhwaldi, Sharp, Alraja) — each captures a single point in time, so none can establish causality or track how acceptance/behavior changes as technology matures or policy is implemented.
   > **Supporting evidence:**
   > 1. "In Jordan, 434 healthcare professionals participated in a cross-sectional online survey that was used to collect data." (Alkhwaldi, 2023, p. 2135)
   > 2. "[T]he cross-sectional design of this research makes it more difficult to prove causality and ascertain the temporal order of events." (Alkhwaldi, 2023, p. 2154)
   > 3. "A cross-sectional survey was conducted with a nationally representative sample (N = 1001) using a random stratified sampling method." (Sharp et al., 2020, p. 802)
   > 4. "A cross-sectional nationally representative household survey of residents aged 16+ years living in Wales UK was conducted in September and October 2017." (Sharp et al., 2020, p. 803)

2. **Small, geographically narrow qualitative samples** — the Sweden AI study (26 leaders) and Coombs' Montana study (12 providers) both draw conclusions from a single region and a small number of informants, limiting generalizability to other health systems or cultures.
   > **Supporting evidence:**
   > 1. "Individual, semi-structured interviews were conducted from October 2020 to May 2021 with 26 healthcare leaders." (Petersson et al., 2022, p. 1)
   > 2. "We used qualitative methods and a convenience sample of healthcare providers who currently practice in the rural US state of Montana. Our sample included 12 healthcare providers from diverse training backgrounds and specialties." (Coombs et al., 2022, p. 1)
   > 3. "[T]he study was conducted in a county council (also...)" — a single regional Swedish setting. (Petersson et al., 2022, p. 3)
   > 4. "Our qualitative study explored rural healthcare providers' views on some of the social, cultural, and programmatic factors that influence access to healthcare among their patient populations." — single-state (Montana) scope. (Coombs et al., 2022, p. 12)

3. **No source measures financial or economic outcomes directly.** None of the 10 articles test whether technology adoption changes actual *spending behavior* (e.g., a shift from reactive to proactive allocation) — they measure attitudes, intentions, or access barriers, not budgetary or economic impact.
   > **Note on evidence:** this is an absence-based finding (the literature does not discuss what it does not measure), so it cannot be directly quoted — it is established by the consistent presence of *attitude/intention/access* framing (see Theme A, B, D quotes above) and the consistent absence of financial-outcome framing across all 10 abstracts and conclusions reviewed.

4. **Emerging-economy contexts are almost entirely absent.** The settings represented are Taiwan, Sweden, Wales, the U.S. (Boston, Montana), and Europe broadly; only Jordan (Alkhwaldi) is arguably a developing-economy case, and even there the framing is about BI acceptance generally, not resource-constrained health infrastructure.
   > **Supporting evidence (settings, as stated by each source):**
   > 1. "A validated survey instrument was administered to 159 IS professionals across Taiwanese hospitals..." (Wu, Chen, & Greenes, 2009, p. 72)
   > 2. "[C]oncerning the implementation of AI in healthcare" in "a regional Swedish healthcare setting." (Petersson et al., 2022, p. 1)
   > 3. "A cross-sectional nationally representative household survey of residents aged 16+ years living in Wales UK..." (Sharp et al., 2020, p. 803)
   > 4. "In Jordan, 434 healthcare professionals participated in a cross-sectional online survey..." — the one partial exception, still framed generically rather than around resource-constrained infrastructure. (Alkhwaldi, 2023, p. 2135)

5. **Scoping reviews bundle multiple interventions together** (Handtke, Lebano), which the authors themselves acknowledge makes it difficult to isolate which specific strategy or component drives an observed effect.
   > **Supporting evidence:**
   > 1. "[T]hese groups are very heterogeneous and their needs and perceived barriers to healthcare may differ substantially." (Handtke, Schilgen, & Mösko, 2019, p. 18 [PDF pagination])
   > 2. Reported effects were "generally positive, but they were often small or not significant, and many studies used several components at once, which made single effects hard to isolate" (per synopsis; see [original_src_files/Selection of Online Academic Journals.docx](../original_src_files/) for the exact wording captured during source selection).
   > 3. "[W]ithout better data on the effectiveness of the measures introduced so far, both the policies addressing patients and those addressing providers" cannot be clearly evaluated. (Lebano et al., 2020, p. 9)
   > 4. "[I]nequalities are the results of legal barriers in access to care for refugees and undocumented migrants, and are also due to the economic situation of migrants" — multiple compounding factors bundled together. (Lebano et al., 2020, p. 9)

## 3. Implications for Future Research

**Are there common sources/frameworks cited across this literature that seem highly influential and worth attention?**
Yes — three frameworks/methods recur and anchor the quantitative half of this literature: the **Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT)** (Alkhwaldi), the **Theory of Planned Behavior combined with Privacy Calculus Theory** (Alraja), and **Partial Least Squares / Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM)** as the dominant statistical approach (Wu, Alkhwaldi, Alraja). Any future research in this space — including the dissertation — should be conversant with these models even if it ultimately takes an empirical-economic-data approach rather than a survey-based acceptance model.

> **Supporting evidence:**
> 1. "Leveraging the theoretical lens of the 'unified theory of acceptance and use of technology' (UTAUT), a study framework was proposed and integrated with three context-related factors..." (Alkhwaldi, 2023, p. 2135)
> 2. "[T]his work assessed gender and generational differences... comprising a synthesis of TPB Ajzen (1991) [and] PCT (Culnan and Armstrong, 1999) and the trust risk framework (Mayer et al., 1995a)..." (Alraja, 2022, p. 2)
> 3. "[F]indings of the 'structural equation modeling' revealed that professionals' behavioral intentions toward using BI systems were significantly affected by performance expectancy, social influence, facilitating conditions..." (Alkhwaldi, 2023, p. 2135)
> 4. "Methods: A validated survey instrument was administered to 159 IS professionals across Taiwanese hospitals, analyzed using Partial Least Squares (PLS) method." (Wu, Chen, & Greenes, 2009, p. 72)

**Are there common conclusions about future research that might be warranted?**
Yes — nearly every technology-acceptance study (Wu, Alkhwaldi, the Sweden AI study, Alraja) concludes by calling for research beyond *intention to adopt*, toward **actual implementation strategy, capacity-building, and regulatory/policy design**. The Sweden AI study is the most explicit: it calls for "laws and policies... to regulate the design and execution of effective AI implementation strategies." This directly supports a policy-focused (rather than purely technical) dissertation angle.

> **Supporting evidence:**
> 1. "The results point to the need to develop implementation strategies across healthcare organisations to address challenges to AI-specific capacity building. Laws and policies are needed to regulate the design and execution of effective AI implementation strategies." (Petersson et al., 2022, p. 1)
> 2. "There is a need to invest time and resources in implementation processes, with collaboration across healthcare, county councils, and industry partnerships." (Petersson et al., 2022, p. 1)
> 3. "[T]he existing laws and policies have not kept pace with technological developments..." (Petersson et al., 2022, p. 4)
> 4. "Concerns were raised as to how errors would be handled when AI systems contributed to decision making, highlighting the need for clear laws and policies." (Petersson et al., 2022, p. 5)

**Are there common errors or oversights that may need to be corrected?**
The clearest recurring oversight is the **complete absence of economic/financial outcome measures**. This body of literature studies *whether* people will adopt technology and *why*, but never *what happens to institutional spending or resource allocation* once adoption occurs. This is precisely the white space the dissertation's research question is positioned to fill. A second oversight: equity-focused research (migrants, rural populations, cultural competence) and technology-adoption research run on **separate tracks** in this literature — none of the 10 sources study whether the populations most affected by structural access barriers are also the ones technology adoption is (or isn't) reaching.

> **Supporting evidence (for the separate-tracks oversight):**
> 1. "[F]or medical supplies used in healthcare units, inventory management systems are reactive." — the one source (Beaulieu & Bentahar) that gets closest to a resource-allocation framing never engages with the equity literature's migrant/rural populations. (Beaulieu & Bentahar, 2021, p. 4)
> 2. "[D]ocumentation status directly shapes eligibility for health coverage..." — the equity literature (Joseph) never engages with the technology-acceptance frameworks (UTAUT/TPB) used elsewhere in this set. (Joseph, health migrations study, p. 5 [PDF pagination])
> 3. "Organisational and administrative issues were highlighted as barriers to access healthcare for migrants..." (Lebano et al., 2020, p. 6) — access barriers are documented, but no technology-adoption lens is applied to them.
> 4. "[H]ealthcare leaders are expected to have a key role in the implementation and use of AI..." (Petersson et al., 2022, p. 3) — implementation-capacity research (technology track) never cross-references the access-equity literature (Handtke, Coombs, Lebano, Joseph).

## 4. Scientific Research Question

> **To what extent does organizational and professional acceptance of data-driven digital health technologies (e.g., business intelligence, artificial intelligence, IoT-enabled platforms) mediate whether healthcare institutions shift from reactive to proactive resource allocation?**

This question is written as a question, is testable (acceptance can be measured via established instruments like UTAUT; resource allocation shifts can be measured via institutional spending/procurement data), is falsifiable (a null result — no relationship between acceptance and allocation behavior — is a possible outcome), and follows naturally from the literature assessed: it directly addresses the gap identified above (acceptance is well-studied, financial/allocation outcomes are not) while building on the acceptance frameworks (UTAUT, TPB) that recur across these 10 sources.

> **Supporting evidence (grounding the RQ directly in the closest precedent found):**
> 1. "[F]or medical supplies used in healthcare units, inventory management systems are reactive." (Beaulieu & Bentahar, 2021, p. 4) — the clearest existing example of "reactive" resource management in this literature, which the RQ proposes testing against technology acceptance.
> 2. "We suggest a roadmap for digitalization to improve the performance of the healthcare supply chain" and "propose a structured digitalization in terms of efforts required and impacts on the healthcare supply chain" (Beaulieu & Bentahar, 2021, p. 1) — establishes that digitalization *can* be framed as a lever for shifting resource management, the same logic the RQ applies to broader technology acceptance.
> 3. "[P]rofessionals' behavioral intentions toward using BI systems were significantly affected by performance expectancy, social influence, facilitating conditions, MLR, RDC and PTA." (Alkhwaldi, 2023, p. 2135) — establishes acceptance as a measurable, modelable construct the RQ can build on.
> 4. "The results point to the need to develop implementation strategies across healthcare organisations..." (Petersson et al., 2022, p. 1) — directly supports moving from acceptance-only research toward the RQ's implementation/resource-allocation framing.

**Relationship to the dissertation's current leading question:** this is a narrower, evidence-grounded stepping stone toward the dissertation's leading research question in [dissertation/research_questions.md](../../../../dissertation/research_questions.md) — "To what extent does the implementation of predictive AI models and interoperable digital health architectures reduce reactive crisis spending in public health interventions within emerging economies?" This Week 4 literature base establishes the acceptance/adoption mechanism; the dissertation question extends it specifically to predictive AI, interoperability, and emerging-economy contexts.
