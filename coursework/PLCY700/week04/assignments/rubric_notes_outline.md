8. **Lebano (2020)** — Migrants'/refugees' health status & healthcare in Europe (scoping review, 77 papers)

10. **Beaulie (2021)** — Digitalization of the healthcare supply chain (roadmap/umbrella review)

## Criterion 1 — Common Elements/Themes (40 pts)
Candidate themes (need 3+, evaluated for significance):
1. **Technology potential consistently outpaces adoption** — nearly every technology-focused source (1, 3, 6, 9, 10) frames its topic as high-potential-but-underused: HTM competency gaps, BI "lack of exploration," AI's "slow and variable uptake," IoT adoption gaps, supply chains that "lag far behind" other industries.
2. **Adoption is gated by human/organizational trust and readiness, not just technical capability** — Wu (1), Alkhwaldi (3), Alraja (9), and the Sweden AI study (6) all identify soft factors (trust, risk perception, professional autonomy, decision-making culture, change-management capacity) as the actual bottleneck, more than the technology itself.
3. **Structural/social barriers drive healthcare inequity independent of technology** — Handtke (2), Coombs (7), Lebano (8), and Joseph (5) converge on the idea that cultural, linguistic, geographic, and legal/administrative barriers — not information gaps — are what block equitable access.
4. **Public/professional acceptance is a precondition for policy success** — Sharp (4) and the Sweden AI leaders study (6) both frame acceptability/acceptance itself as the dependent variable that determines whether a policy or technology succeeds at all.

## Criterion 2 — Gaps/Weaknesses (30 pts)
Candidate gaps (need 3+, evaluated for significance):
1. **Cross-sectional design dominates** (3, 4, 9) — snapshot surveys can show association but not causality or change over time.
2. **Small, geographically narrow qualitative samples** (6: N=26 Sweden; 7: N=12 Montana) — findings may not generalize beyond a single region/health system.
3. **No source measures financial/economic outcomes directly** — none of the 10 test whether technology adoption actually changes *spending patterns* (reactive vs. proactive) — a direct gap relative to the dissertation's leading research question.
4. **Almost no emerging-economy context** — settings are Taiwan, Sweden, Wales, Jordan, US/Boston, Montana, and Europe broadly; only Jordan (3) is arguably a developing-economy case, and even that isn't the primary lens of the study.
5. **Bundled interventions in scoping reviews** (2, 8) — multiple components tested together make it hard to isolate which single strategy drives an effect.

## Criterion 3 — Implications for Future Research (30 pts)
Candidate implications (need 3+):
1. **Influential/recurring frameworks worth building on:** UTAUT (3), Theory of Planned Behavior + Privacy Calculus Theory (9), PLS/SEM as the dominant quantitative method (1, 3, 9) — these are the theoretical/methodological backbone this literature keeps returning to.
2. **Recurring call for implementation-level research, not just intention-level research** — 1, 3, 6, and 9 all study *intent to adopt*, but conclude that organizations need research on actual implementation strategy, capacity-building, and regulation (explicit in 6).
3. **Research trend toward economic/financial framing is conspicuously absent** — none of the 10 sources reframe technology adoption in terms of cost/spending shifts; this is a clear white space the dissertation's research question can occupy.
4. **Equity-focused sources (2, 5, 7, 8) suggest future work should pair technology-adoption research with access-barrier research** — currently they run on separate tracks in this literature.
