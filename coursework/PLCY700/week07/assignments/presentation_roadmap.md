## 3. Section-by-Section Talking Points

### A. Hook + Topic Introduction (0:00–0:45)
**Goal:** Grab attention, state your policy area clearly, establish currency/urgency.

Key points to hit, in your own words:
- Your topic: the "Leapfrog Advantage & AI Integration" angle — predictive AI + interoperable digital health infrastructure in emerging economies.
- **Real-world currency hook (new, from the Mexico case):** Mention that this isn't hypothetical — Mexico just made digital health records and interoperability legally mandatory as of January 2026, with full inter-institutional data exchange required by January 2027. Frame the open question: is a legal mandate alone enough, or does it take more?
- Keep this section under 45 seconds — it's a hook, not a literature summary.

### B. Central Thesis / Research Angle (0:45–1:45)
**Goal:** Explain why emerging economies are uniquely positioned, and what gap your research targets.

Key points:
- Emerging economies lack legacy health IT systems that developed nations are stuck with — this is a structural *advantage*, not just a disadvantage, letting them build agile, interoperable architectures from scratch ("leapfrogging").
- The core gap you're targeting: monitoring & evaluation (M&E) is almost always bolted on as an afterthought in health policy, not designed in from the start — which is why crises get managed reactively instead of prevented proactively.

### C. Common Themes (1:45–2:45) — pick 2–3 of these four
From your Week 4 Literature Assessment (cite author/year as you speak):
1. **Technology's potential consistently outpaces real-world adoption** — e.g., Alkhwaldi (2023) notes business intelligence has "grown in popularity" but suffers a "lack of exploration" in healthcare; Beaulieu & Bentahar (2021) state the healthcare supply chain "lags far behind" other industries in digitalization.
2. **Adoption is gated by trust/organizational readiness, not technical capability** — e.g., Alkhwaldi (2023) found clinicians perceive BI systems as threats to professional autonomy; Petersson et al. (2022) found that if healthcare professionals don't trust AI outputs, they won't use them.
3. **Structural/social barriers drive inequity independent of technology** — Coombs et al. (2022) on rural healthcare friction; Lebano et al. (2020) on migrant/refugee access barriers.
4. **Acceptance itself is treated as the dependent variable of success** — Sharp et al. (2020): "For public health policies to be effective, it is critical that they are acceptable to the public."

**Recommendation:** Lead with #2 and #4 — they set up your "implementation is the real constraint" framing better than #1 or #3 alone.

### D. Gaps / Weaknesses (2:45–3:30) — pick 2–3
1. **Cross-sectional designs dominate** — single point in time, can't establish causality (Alkhwaldi, 2023; Sharp et al., 2020).
2. **Small, geographically narrow samples** — Sweden study (26 leaders), Montana study (12 providers) — limited generalizability.
3. **No source measures economic/financial outcomes directly** — the literature studies attitudes and intentions, never actual spending behavior. **This is your single most important gap to state clearly — it's the exact white space your RQ fills.**
4. **Emerging-economy contexts are almost entirely absent** — settings are Taiwan, Sweden, Wales, U.S.; only Jordan partially qualifies.

**Recommendation:** Lead with #3 and #4 together — they directly set up why your research question is novel.

### E. Implications + Efficiency vs. Equity Tension (3:30–4:15)
Key points:
- The literature itself calls for research to move beyond "will they adopt it?" toward actual implementation strategy and policy design (Petersson et al., 2022: "laws and policies are needed to regulate the design and execution of effective AI implementation strategies").
- **Name the efficiency vs. equity tension explicitly** (this hits the Week 7 learning outcome directly): predictive AI/interoperable infrastructure is an *efficiency* play — less waste, better-targeted spending — but infrastructure investment can be *inequitable* if deployed first in wealthier or urban regions, widening the gap for rural/marginalized populations.
- **Real-world closing tie-in (new, from the Mexico case):** Mexico's 2026 mandate is already running into exactly the capability gaps — funding, connectivity, training — that policy theory predicts will determine whether a legal mandate alone actually works. Your research will test that relationship directly.

### F. Research Question + Why It Matters (4:15–5:00)
State clearly and slowly (consider putting this on a slide verbatim):

> "To what extent does the implementation of predictive AI models and interoperable digital health architectures reduce reactive crisis spending in public health interventions within emerging economies?"

Close with why it matters:
- It fills the white space identified in the literature: it measures *economic* outcomes, it's set in *emerging* economies, and it tests a *policy intervention* — not just adoption attitudes.
- If emerging economies can shift from crisis-driven to data-driven spending, that changes both their fiscal capacity and offers a model for other low-resource health systems globally.

## 4. Causal Theory Framing (Core Week 7 Concept — Make Sure This Is Explicit Somewhere)
Say the chain out loud, explicitly, at some point (works well woven into section B or E):
- **Cause/problem:** M&E is bolted onto health policy as an afterthought, so agencies can't see crises coming — they only react once a crisis is already underway.
- **Intervention:** Predictive AI models + interoperable digital health architecture, designed in from the start.
- **Effect:** A measurable shift from reactive to proactive/targeted spending.

This is what the Week 7 quiz and "evaluate causal theories" learning outcome are testing — don't just restate Week 4's themes without this explicit cause → intervention → effect framing somewhere in the video.
