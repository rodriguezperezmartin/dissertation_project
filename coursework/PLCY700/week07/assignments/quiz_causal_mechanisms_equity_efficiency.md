## Part II: Essay / Short Answer Questions

### Question 11: Equity vs. Efficiency

**Prompt:** Think about a policy of interest to you. Then, consider how you might go about measuring its equity. What about its efficiency? Are these necessarily in conflict? Why or why not?

**Response:**
Given that my interest in public policy lies in digital infrastructures to expand the reach of healthcare in emerging economies, I would like to explore possible means of measuring equity and efficiency in this space.

Measuring the equity of this policy requires assessing how access, digital resources, and health outcomes are distributed across different segments of the population. Reflecting on Deborah Stone's multidimensional framework of equity, an evaluator attempting to measure fairness through a single, uniform standard will not succeed. Instead, equity must be evaluated by whether marginalized, low-income, and rural populations gain proportional access to broadband and hardware, whether care is prioritized based on clinical need and illness severity rather than geographic wealth, and whether the administrative process of enrollment is transparent and barrier-free. If predictive tools are concentrated solely in affluent urban hospital systems, the policy fails the equity test by widening existing health disparities.

Measuring the efficiency of this digital health policy, by contrast, focuses on the input-to-output ratio: maximizing health outcomes and cost savings for a given investment of public capital. From an economic perspective, efficiency should be evaluated through cost-effectiveness metrics, such as reductions in emergency room visits, fewer preventable hospital readmissions per dollar spent, or the lowering of administrative overhead through automated health records. As Stone points out, defining efficiency is inherently political because it hinges on what outputs are valued and whose costs are counted—such as determining whether the financial savings accrued to the state outweigh the burden placed on patients to acquire digital literacy.

Ultimately, equity and efficiency are not inherently in conflict. In classical welfare economics, policies aimed at redistributing resources are frequently criticized for creating administrative friction and deadweight loss. Yet, as Stone demonstrates within the polis, equity and efficiency can be mutually reinforcing. Ensuring equitable, proactive health access for underserved communities directly improves long-term systemic efficiency by mitigating chronic disease progression before it escalates into catastrophic, expensive emergency care funded by public budgets.

### Question 12: Causal Theory in Policy Design

**Prompt:** Why is it essential for policy makers to have a causal theory when creating a policy design?

**Response:**
According to our readings and drawing from the coursework up to this point, it is crucial for policymakers to present sound causal theories because without them, they cannot establish credible logical connections between the selected policy tools and the desired outcomes. If an underlying causal chain misdiagnoses the true root cause of a problem, even the best-funded programs will fail. These will generate unintended consequences and waste scarce public resources by treating symptoms rather than structural drivers. In addition, Stone demonstrated that causal theories are political instruments that help to define responsibility, assign blame, and justify governmental interventions. Causal theories help ensure that administrative implementations are technically feasible and well-targeted, while also helping to hold institutions accountable for results.
