# McClellan, *Liberty, Order, and Justice* — The Founding Documents (Appendices C, D, E)

**Citation (APA 7):** McClellan, J. (2000). *Liberty, order, and justice: An introduction to the constitutional principles of American government* (3rd ed.). Liberty Fund.
**Sections:** Pages 171–187 (Appendix C: Declaration and Resolves of the First Continental Congress; Appendix D: Declaration of the Causes and Necessity of Taking Up Arms; Appendix E: The Declaration of Independence)
**Relevance:** Week 3 Discussion Thread — Traces the rapid evolution of the colonists' definition of liberty from a set of inherited British legal privileges to a universal, natural right granted by God.

---

## Executive Summary

These three appendices contain primary source documents that map the escalation of the American constitutional argument from 1774 to 1776. The *Resolves of the First Continental Congress* demonstrates the colonists initially fighting to preserve their traditional English rights against Parliamentary overreach. The *Declaration of the Causes and Necessity of Taking Up Arms* shows the colonists escalating to physical force to prevent political enslavement, though still claiming loyalty to the Crown. Finally, the *Declaration of Independence* represents a complete paradigm shift: liberty is redefined not as a British inheritance, but as a universal, unalienable natural right, necessitating a complete severing of ties and the creation of a new sovereign people.

## Section 1: Declaration and Resolves of the First Continental Congress (1774) (pp. 171–?)

### Core Concept: Liberty as an Inherited Legal Right

Drafted in response to the Coercive (Intolerable) Acts, this document outlines the colonists' baseline constitutional arguments before the outbreak of war.

**Key features:**
- **The Foundation of Rights:** The document explicitly claims rights based on three pillars: the immutable laws of nature, the principles of the English constitution, and the several colonial charters.
- **Specific Protections Claimed:** It demands the rights to life, liberty, and property; the right to participate in their own legislative councils; the right to a trial by a jury of peers from the local vicinage; and the right to peaceably assemble and petition the King.
- **The Threat Protested:** The primary grievances include the keeping of a standing army in times of peace without local consent and Parliament's assumption of the power to legislate for the colonies in "all cases whatsoever."

### Key Insight for Week 3 Discussion

In 1774, the definition of liberty was still deeply conservative and institutional. The colonists were not trying to invent a new utopia; they were desperately trying to protect the traditional "ordered liberty" of their local assemblies from being dismantled by a centralized Parliament.

## Section 2: Declaration of the Causes and Necessity of Taking Up Arms (1775)

### Core Concept: Liberty Requires Active Defense Against Tyranny

Drafted by Thomas Jefferson and John Dickinson shortly after the battles of Lexington and Concord, this document justifies armed resistance.

**Key features:**
- **The Choice: Enslavement or Resistance:** The authors frame the conflict as a binary choice between unconditional submission to the tyranny of irritated ministers (political enslavement) or resistance by force.
- **Preservation, Not Separation (Yet):** Crucially, the document explicitly states that the colonists do not yet mean to dissolve the union with Britain. They are taking up arms strictly in self-defense to preserve their inherited liberties.
- **Liberty as a Sacred Trust:** The document utilizes highly moral and religious language, framing the defense of liberty as a divine obligation to ensure future generations are not born into political slavery.

### Key Insight for Week 3 Discussion

This reveals the colonial understanding of the relationship between liberty and authority. When a governing authority stops protecting liberty and instead actively seeks to destroy property and self-governance, the people have a moral duty to resist. Authority is only legitimate as long as it honors the original constitutional covenant.

## Section 3: The Declaration of Independence (1776) (pp. ?–187)

### Core Concept: Liberty as an Unalienable Natural Right

The final break. The argument shifts completely from British constitutional law to universal natural law.

**Key features:**
- **The Shift in the Definition of Liberty:** Liberty is no longer defined merely as the "Rights of Englishmen." It is an unalienable right endowed by the Creator, applicable to all people.
- **The Purpose of Government:** Governments are instituted solely to secure these natural rights (Life, Liberty, and the Pursuit of Happiness), deriving their just powers entirely from the consent of the governed.
- **The Indictment of the Executive:** While earlier documents primarily blamed Parliament, the Declaration of Independence aims its grievances directly at King George III. The King is accused of destroying the constitutional order by refusing assent to necessary laws, dissolving local representative houses, making judges dependent on his will, and establishing arbitrary military rule.

### Key Insight for Week 3 Discussion

This document answers the prompt regarding how earlier understandings of liberty compare to today. The Declaration of Independence provided the modern American definition of liberty as a universal, pre-political human right. However, unlike modern hyper-individualism, the colonists immediately followed this declaration of liberty by mutually pledging to each other their lives, fortunes, and sacred honor — proving that liberty still required deep communal obligation and sacrifice.

## Comparative Note: The Evolution of Liberty (1774 vs. 1776)

| Dimension | 1774 (First Continental Congress) | 1776 (Declaration of Independence) |
|---|---|---|
| **Source of liberty** | English Constitution, common law, and colonial charters | The Laws of Nature and Nature's God |
| **Goal** | Reconciliation, preservation of the British Empire, restoration of old rights | Complete separation, independent statehood, establishment of a new republic |
| **Primary antagonist** | The British Parliament (for legislating without representation) | King George III (for acting as a tyrant and breaking the social contract) |

### Questions Raised for Today (per Week 3 Discussion Prompt)

1. **Inherited vs. Universal Rights:** The colonists shifted from defending specific, historically rooted rights (1774) to claiming universal, abstract natural rights (1776). Today, do we rely too heavily on abstract concepts of liberty while neglecting the practical, institutional mechanisms (like local juries and decentralized assemblies) that actually enforce those rights?
2. **The Threshold for Resistance:** The colonists believed that a long train of administrative abuses and usurpations justified altering or abolishing their government. How do modern Americans evaluate when government overreach transitions from a mere policy disagreement into a fundamental threat to constitutional liberty?
