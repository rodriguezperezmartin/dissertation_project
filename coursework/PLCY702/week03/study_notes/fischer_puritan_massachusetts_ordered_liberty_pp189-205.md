# Fischer, *Albion's Seed* — Massachusetts (Puritans): Ordered Liberty

**Citation (APA 7):** Fischer, D. H. (1989). *Albion's seed: Four British folkways in America*. Oxford University Press.
**Pages:** 189–205
**Sections:** "Massachusetts Order Ways: The Puritan Idea of Order as Unity" (pp. 189–194); "Massachusetts Power Ways: The Politics of Town Meeting Government" (pp. 195–199); "Massachusetts Freedom Ways: The Puritan Idea of Ordered Liberty" (pp. 200–205)
**Folkway:** East Anglian Puritans → Massachusetts Bay Colony (1629–1640s migration wave)
**Relevance:** Week 3 Discussion Thread — Subculture #1 option (Puritan New England)

---

## Executive Summary

Fischer argues that the Massachusetts Puritans built their entire political and social order around a single organizing principle: **unity**. For the Puritans, order was not merely the absence of chaos — it was the active, deliberate binding of individuals into a single moral community under God. This conception of order (pp. 189–194) directly shaped their system of town meeting government (pp. 195–199), which in turn produced a highly distinctive understanding of liberty: not freedom *from* restraint, but freedom *to do what is morally right* within a tightly bound community (pp. 200–205). This is the famous concept of **"ordered liberty"** — a liberty that only has meaning inside a framework of communal and divine obligation.

## Section 1: Massachusetts Order Ways — The Puritan Idea of Order as Unity (pp. 189–194)

### Core Concept: Order as Unity, Not Hierarchy

Unlike the Virginia Cavaliers (who understood order as hierarchy — see companion study notes), the Puritans understood order as **organic unity**. Society was conceived as a single body, bound together by a **covenant** — first with God, then with one another.

**Key features (pp. 189–194):**
- **Covenant theology as the foundation of social order:** Every Puritan community was constituted by an explicit covenant (church covenant, town covenant) in which members voluntarily bound themselves to God and to each other. This was not a metaphor — covenants were formally drafted, signed, and renewed.
- **Order as "knitting together":** Fischer cites Puritan language describing the ideal community as being "knit together" into "one body" — order meant the elimination of faction, dissent, and individual deviation from the shared moral project.
- **Uniformity as a virtue:** Unlike later American pluralism, the Puritans deliberately sought **religious and moral uniformity** within their towns. Dissenters (e.g., Anne Hutchinson, Roger Williams) were banished — not because the Puritans misunderstood toleration, but because *their definition of order depended on unity*, and visible dissent was a direct threat to the colony's foundational structure.
- **The "due form of government":** Order required a formal, divinely sanctioned structure of authority (magistrates, ministers, freemen) — but this structure existed to *serve* and *protect* the covenanted community, not to dominate it for its own sake.

### Key Insight for Week 3 Discussion

Order-as-unity explains why Puritan liberty could never be understood as individual autonomy: if the entire society is one covenanted body, individual liberty untethered from the group's moral purpose would be a kind of self-amputation — a threat to the body's integrity, not an expression of freedom.

## Section 2: Massachusetts Power Ways — The Politics of Town Meeting Government (pp. 195–199)

### Core Concept: Decentralized, Participatory, but Restricted Governance

**Key features (pp. 195–199):**
- **The town meeting:** The foundational unit of Massachusetts political power was the town meeting — a direct-democracy assembly where "freemen" gathered to make decisions on local matters (land allocation, taxation, minister selection, local ordinances).
- **Restricted suffrage — "visible saints":** Crucially, political participation (freemanship) was generally restricted to **full, examined members of the Puritan church** — the so-called "visible saints." This was not a secular democracy; political power was explicitly fused with religious qualification.
- **The General Court:** At the colony level, the Massachusetts General Court combined elected deputies (House of Deputies, chosen by town freemen) with magistrates (the Governor's Council), creating a bicameral structure that balanced broad local participation with elite religious/political authority.
- **Annual elections and rotation:** Officers were elected annually, providing accountability and limiting the entrenchment of any single leader's power — but within a system still gatekept by church membership.
- **Local autonomy within colonial unity:** Towns had significant self-governing authority over local affairs, while still operating under the overarching covenant-based unity of the colony as a whole (connecting back to Order Ways).

### Key Insight for Week 3 Discussion

The town meeting demonstrates that Puritan liberty was fundamentally **participatory and communal**, not a private sphere protected from government. Political power was *exercised through* collective deliberation, not *protected from* collective authority — a crucial contrast with modern liberal/libertarian conceptions of liberty as a shield against government.

## Section 3: Massachusetts Freedom Ways — The Puritan Idea of Ordered Liberty (pp. 200–205)

### Core Concept: "Ordered Liberty" — Freedom to Do What Is Right, Not What One Wills

This is the section most directly relevant to the Week 3 discussion prompt.

**Key features (pp. 200–205):**
- **Winthrop's "Little Speech on Liberty" (1645):** Fischer centers this section on John Winthrop's famous distinction between two kinds of liberty:
  1. **Natural liberty** — the liberty of man "in reference to brute creatures," the capacity to do whatever one has the power or will to do, "evil as well as good." Winthrop explicitly calls this kind of liberty "incompatible... with authority" and warns it leads to licentiousness.
  2. **Civil/moral/"ordered" liberty** — the liberty "to that only which is good, just, and honest." This is the liberty "which Christ hath made us free," exercised in subjection to authority. Winthrop argues a person must willingly submit to lawful authority to possess *this* kind of liberty, comparing it to a wife's submission to a husband "not Subjection but a kind of liberty."
- **Liberty requires authority, not opposition to it:** For the Puritans, true liberty is not threatened by government — it is actually *enabled* by submission to righteous, covenant-based authority. Liberty and authority are not in tension; they are mutually constitutive.
- **Liberty as a communal, not individual, possession:** Because liberty is defined as "doing good," and "good" is determined collectively through the church-covenant community, liberty cannot be fully private. An individual's liberty is inseparable from the community's moral standards.
- **Practical consequence — intolerance as liberty-preservation:** From inside this framework, banishing dissenters (Hutchinson, Williams) was not seen as a *violation* of liberty — it was seen as *protecting* the colony's "ordered liberty" from corrupting "natural liberty" (licentiousness) that would destroy the covenant community's moral foundation.

### Key Insight for Week 3 Discussion

This is the clearest colonial-era articulation of **liberty as inseparable from community and authority** — the opposite of a purely negative, "freedom from government" conception. Winthrop's framework directly answers the discussion prompt's question about "the way liberty and community, or liberty and authority, have been related."

## Comparative Note: Puritan "Ordered Liberty" vs. Modern American Liberty

| Dimension | Puritan "Ordered Liberty" (1630s–40s) | Modern American Liberty |
|---|---|---|
| **Source of liberty** | Granted/defined by God and covenant community | Inherent/natural right, often framed as pre-political |
| **Relationship to authority** | Liberty requires submission to legitimate authority | Liberty is often defined as freedom *from* government overreach |
| **Individual vs. communal** | Liberty is a collective/communal possession | Liberty is primarily an individual possession |
| **Moral content** | Liberty = freedom to do good (not do as one wills) | Liberty often includes freedom to do as one wills, within legal limits |
| **Tolerance of dissent** | Low — dissent threatens the covenant's unity | High — dissent protected as exercise of liberty |
| **Continuity today** | Still visible in: communitarian political theory, arguments for virtue-based "ordered liberty" in constitutional interpretation (e.g., certain originalist/natural-law arguments), religious-liberty communities | Dominant in: classical liberalism, libertarianism, most modern rights discourse |

### Questions Raised for Today (per Week 3 Discussion Prompt)

1. If liberty is conceived purely as freedom *from* restraint (the modern default), does American political culture lose the Puritan insight that community bonds and shared moral purpose can *enable* rather than merely *constrain* freedom?
2. Winthrop's distinction between "natural liberty" (license) and "civil liberty" (ordered freedom) anticipates ongoing debates about whether unlimited personal autonomy actually serves human flourishing, or whether some restraint/commitment to shared values is necessary for genuine liberty.
3. Does the Puritan model's intolerance of dissent reveal an inherent tension in any liberty-through-unity framework — i.e., can a community ever fully reconcile shared moral purpose with individual freedom of conscience?

## Capstone Connection

The Puritan "ordered liberty" framework — where individual/local freedom operates *within* a shared covenant structure rather than *against* central authority — offers an interesting parallel to community health governance models. Guatemala's decentralized digital health strategy succeeds in part because it builds on **existing community/provider relationships** (a "covenant-like" buy-in) rather than imposing top-down compliance. This mirrors the Puritan insight that **authority is more durable and legitimate when covenant-based (consensual, locally rooted) rather than purely coercive.**
