# McClellan, *Liberty, Order, and Justice* — Local Government, Civil Liberties, and Independence

**Citation (APA 7):** McClellan, J. (2000). *Liberty, order, and justice: An introduction to the constitutional principles of American government* (3rd ed.). Liberty Fund.
**Sections:** Pages 107–120 ("Local Government in the Colonies," "Civil Liberties in the Colonies," "The Movement Toward Independence")
**Relevance:** Week 3 Discussion Thread — Identifies the specific constitutional features protecting liberty (local government, common law) and the specific threats to liberty protested by colonists (taxation without representation, administrative overreach).

---

## Executive Summary

In this section, McClellan transitions from the origins of colonial government to the practical mechanisms that protected colonial liberty. He argues that American liberty was not a sudden invention but an inheritance of English common law, preserved and adapted through decentralized local governments. When the British Parliament abandoned its policy of "salutary neglect" and attempted to impose direct taxation and centralized administrative control, the colonists viewed this not merely as an economic burden, but as a fundamental constitutional threat to their liberty. This structural conflict over who had the authority to tax and govern at the local level ignited the movement toward independence.

## Section 1: Local Government and Civil Liberties in the Colonies (pp. 107–114)

### Core Concept: Liberty Guarded by Decentralization and the Common Law

McClellan emphasizes that the day-to-day administration of the colonies was highly decentralized, and civil liberties were understood primarily as inherited, historical rights.

**Key features (pp. 107–114):**
- **Structural Decentralization (The Constitutional Feature):** Local government varied by region, reflecting Fischer's folkways. New England relied on the township and town meetings (highly democratic), while the Southern colonies relied on the county system managed by justices of the peace (more aristocratic). Both systems, however, kept administrative power strictly localized.
- **The Rights of Englishmen:** Colonists did not initially argue for "natural rights" or universal human rights. They argued for the "Rights of Englishmen" derived from the Magna Carta, the Petition of Right, and the English Bill of Rights. Liberty was viewed as a specific legal inheritance.
- **Common Law Protections:** Liberty was protected through the practical application of the common law, most notably the right to trial by a local jury, protections for private property, and the emerging freedom of the press (highlighted by the landmark 1735 Zenger trial, which established that truth is a defense against libel).
- **Religious Liberty's Evolution:** Religious liberty was not universally guaranteed early on. Several colonies had established, tax-supported churches. However, the sheer diversity of religious sects in America eventually made strict uniformity impossible, fostering a gradual, pragmatic shift toward toleration.

### Key Insight for Week 3 Discussion

When discussing *constitutional features designed to protect liberty*, you can point directly to McClellan's analysis of the decentralized local courts and the common law jury system. The colonists believed that liberty could only be preserved if justice was administered locally by one's peers, rather than by distant imperial judges.

## Section 2: The Movement Toward Independence (pp. 114–120)

### Core Concept: Centralization as the Primary Threat to Liberty

The conflict with Britain was fundamentally a constitutional crisis regarding sovereignty and representation.

**Key features (pp. 114–120):**
- **Salutary Neglect Ends:** For over a century, Britain largely ignored the internal affairs of the colonies. Following the French and Indian War, Parliament attempted to tighten its grip to pay down war debts.
- **Taxation Without Representation:** Acts such as the Stamp Act and Townshend Acts were viewed as unconstitutional. The colonists argued that under the English constitution, property could not be taken (taxed) without the consent of the people's elected representatives.
- **Actual vs. Virtual Representation:** Parliament claimed the colonists were "virtually represented" because Parliament acted for the good of the whole empire. The colonists, accustomed to local assemblies, demanded "actual representation" — lawmakers must be directly elected by the communities they tax.
- **The Threat to Liberty:** The colonists protested these acts because if Parliament could tax them without their consent, it could effectively destroy their property and local autonomy. Liberty, to the colonists, required the absolute right to control their own internal legislation and property.

### Key Insight for Week 3 Discussion

This section perfectly answers the prompt regarding *threats to liberty protested by colonial Americans*. The primary threat was an external, centralized power attempting to bypass the local assemblies to extract wealth and enforce laws without local consent.

## Capstone Connection

The colonial rejection of "virtual representation" has a direct parallel in modern public policy and technology deployment in emerging economies. When global health organizations design centralized digital infrastructure (like a top-down MLOps architecture or rigid UTAUT-based adoption models), they often operate under a framework of "virtual representation" — assuming they know what is best for the local clinical end-user without consulting them. Just as the colonists rejected Parliament's top-down mandates because they bypassed local governance, healthcare IT projects face massive user resistance when they fail to grant local stakeholders "actual representation" in the design and data governance phases.
