# Alexis de Tocqueville, *Democracy in America* — Vol. 1, Pt. 1, Ch. 5: Necessity of Studying What Happens in the Individual States Before Speaking of the Government of the Union

**Citation (APA 7):** Tocqueville, A. de. (2012). *Democracy in America: In two volumes* (E. Nolla, Ed., & J. T. Schleifer, Trans.). Liberty Fund. (Original work published 1835).
**Sections:** Volume 1, Part 1, Chapter 5 (opening/methodological section)
**Relevance:** Week 4 — Tocqueville's methodological argument for why federalism must be studied from the bottom up — states and townships first, the national Union second.

---

## Executive Summary

Before Tocqueville analyzes the federal Constitution of the United States in later chapters, he pauses to justify a crucial methodological choice: he insists that no reader can understand the American Union correctly without *first* understanding the political life of the individual states — and no one can understand the states without understanding the township. This opening section of Chapter 5 is Tocqueville's argument for a **"bottom-up" theory of federalism**: the Union is not a self-sufficient entity that can be analyzed in isolation; it is a derivative structure, built on top of (and dependent on) the pre-existing political vitality of its constituent parts. This directly complements Week 4's focus on the imperial and confederal roots of American federalism: the Union was built on states that already possessed fully functioning governments, laws, and civic habits.

---

## Section 1: Tocqueville's Methodological Argument

### Core Concept: You Cannot Understand the Whole Without the Parts

- Tocqueville observes that most readers and even many political scientists of his day wanted to begin their study of America with its most famous and impressive feature: the federal Constitution of 1787. He argues this is backwards.
- His reasoning: the federal government is a **relatively recent and comparatively limited creation**, layered on top of state governments that are older, more complete, and touch citizens' daily lives far more directly than the distant national government ever could.
- To understand why the Union took the particular shape it did — a government of carefully enumerated, limited powers, leaving vast reserved authority to the states — one must first understand what kind of political communities the states already were *before* 1787: fully formed republics with their own constitutions, courts, legislatures, and administrative traditions stretching back to the colonial period.

### Key Insight for Week 4 Discussion

This is precisely the argument McClellan makes from a historical-legal angle (the Articles of Confederation and the "imperial roots" of federalism) and that Dr. Ross makes in the Week 4 video ("in the beginning, America was Federal"): the Union did not create the states — the states, already fully formed, created the Union, and only on terms they found acceptable.

---

## Section 2: The State as the Primary Unit of Political Life

### Core Concept: States Are Not Administrative Subdivisions — They Are Complete Political Societies

- Tocqueville stresses that American states, unlike the provinces or departments of centralized European nations (notably France), were **not creations of the central government**. They predated the Union and voluntarily delegated a narrow, enumerated slice of their sovereignty to it.
- Each state retains what Tocqueville calls a nearly complete apparatus of sovereignty: its own constitution, its own elected legislature and executive, its own judiciary, and — critically — its own direct relationship with its citizens through local institutions (counties, townships).
- Because the states possess this independent, "complete" political existence, Tocqueville insists that understanding American government requires scholars to descend *below* the federal level, through the state level, down to the township — the most local unit — before ascending back up to make sense of the federal superstructure.

### Key Insight for Week 4 Discussion

This methodological sequencing (township → county → state → union) is the organizing structure of Tocqueville's entire analysis of American federalism, and it is the reason his observations on local self-government (covered in Week 3's reading of Chapter 5's township sections) logically precede his treatment of the national government in later chapters.

---

## Section 3: Why This Matters for Federalism as a Concept

### Core Concept: Federalism as Derivative, Not Original, Authority

- Because the states pre-existed the Union and voluntarily ceded only specific, enumerated powers to it, Tocqueville's framing supports the theory (echoed throughout Week 4's readings) that **federal authority is inherently limited and delegated**, not an independent or self-originating sovereign power.
- This stands in sharp contrast to a unitary or centralized state (like France), where local administrative units are mere creatures of the national government, possessing only the authority the center chooses to delegate downward.
- Tocqueville's ordering — states first, Union second — is therefore not merely a stylistic or pedagogical choice; it is a substantive claim about where sovereignty and legitimate political authority actually originate in the American system.

### Key Insight for Week 4 Discussion

Tocqueville's methodological point gives you an analytical lens for McClellan's "imperial roots" reading: just as the colonies (soon-to-be states) were already fully formed political communities under the British imperial system before 1776, they remained fully formed political communities under the Articles of Confederation and the Constitution. The Union, at every stage, has been built *on top of*, not *instead of*, functioning state governments.

---

## Comparative Note: Tocqueville's Bottom-Up Method vs. a Top-Down (Unitary) Approach

| Dimension | Tocqueville's Federal/Bottom-Up View | Unitary/Centralized View (e.g., France) |
|---|---|---|
| **Origin of local government authority** | Pre-exists the central government; voluntarily delegates upward | Created by and subordinate to the central government |
| **Order of analysis** | Township → county → state → union | Central government → administrative subdivisions |
| **Source of legitimacy** | Diffused across many self-governing communities | Concentrated in the central sovereign |
| **Risk** | Potential for fragmentation, weak national coordination | Potential for administrative tyranny, loss of local liberty (see Week 3 notes) |

## Capstone Connection

Tocqueville's insistence on understanding the "parts" before the "whole" is directly applicable to comparative health-systems research in emerging economies. A researcher studying national digital health infrastructure mandates (e.g., Mexico's 2026 mandate) cannot adequately explain adoption outcomes by examining only the national policy text — following Tocqueville's method, one must first study the pre-existing political and administrative capacity of the sub-national health authorities (departmental, state, or municipal health systems) that the national mandate is layered on top of. Guatemala's more decentralized digital health approach, by contrast, may reflect a political structure where sub-national health authorities retain significant pre-existing autonomy — precisely the "state before Union" dynamic Tocqueville describes.
