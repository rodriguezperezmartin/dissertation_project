# James McClellan, *Liberty, Order, and Justice* — The Road to Philadelphia (pp. 230–240)

**Citation (APA 7):** McClellan, J. (2000). *Liberty, order, and justice: An introduction to the constitutional principles of American government* (3rd ed.). Liberty Fund.
**Pages:** 230–240
**Relevance:** Week 4 — The crisis of the 1780s and the chain of events that led from the failures of the Articles of Confederation to the calling of the Constitutional Convention.

---

## Executive Summary

This short but pivotal section bridges the Confederation's structural weaknesses (covered in pp. 121–163) and the Constitutional Convention of 1787. McClellan narrates the sequence of interstate meetings and crises — the **Mount Vernon Conference (1785)**, the **Annapolis Convention (1786)**, and **Shays's Rebellion (1786–1787)** — that convinced nationalist-minded leaders (Madison, Hamilton, Washington) that the Articles required not mere amendment but wholesale reconstruction. The section closes with Congress's reluctant authorization of a convention "for the sole and express purpose of revising the Articles of Confederation," a mandate the Philadelphia delegates would ultimately exceed.

---

## Section 1: The Mount Vernon Conference (1785)

### Core Concept: Interstate Commercial Disputes as the Catalyst

- Virginia and Maryland delegates met at George Washington's Mount Vernon estate in March 1785 to resolve navigation and fishing rights disputes on the Potomac River and Chesapeake Bay (McClellan, pp. 230–232).
- The conference succeeded in producing a workable interstate compact, but its larger significance was **demonstrating that even simple commercial coordination between just two neighboring states required direct negotiation** — the Confederation Congress had no mechanism to mediate or regulate such disputes.
- The success of this limited meeting encouraged Virginia to propose a broader interstate conference to address commercial regulation across all the states.

### Key Insight for Week 4 Discussion

Mount Vernon illustrates, in miniature, the commercial chaos McClellan describes in the earlier Confederation chapter: without a central regulatory authority, even friendly neighboring states needed ad hoc diplomacy to manage basic economic coexistence.

---

## Section 2: The Annapolis Convention (1786)

### Core Concept: A Failed Meeting with Consequential Results

- Twelve states were invited to send delegates to Annapolis, Maryland, in September 1786 to discuss uniform commercial regulations among the states. **Only five states** sent delegates, far short of a quorum for substantive action (McClellan, pp. 232–235).
- Rather than disband in failure, the delegates present — including **Alexander Hamilton** and **James Madison** — drafted a report calling for a broader convention the following year in Philadelphia, with an expanded mandate: not just commerce, but a general review of "the exigencies of the Union."
- This report, forwarded to the Confederation Congress and the state legislatures, became the formal basis for the 1787 Convention.

### Key Insight for Week 4 Discussion

The Annapolis Convention's "failure" was strategically productive: a small group of committed nationalists used the moment of institutional embarrassment to build momentum for a much larger constitutional reconstruction than a simple trade conference could have achieved.

---

## Section 3: Shays's Rebellion (1786–1787)

### Core Concept: Economic Distress Exposes the Confederation's Impotence

- In western Massachusetts, heavily indebted farmers — many of them Revolutionary War veterans — faced aggressive tax collection and property foreclosures amid a severe post-war economic depression (McClellan, pp. 235–238).
- Led by Daniel Shays, armed bands of farmers forcibly shut down county courts to prevent foreclosure proceedings and marched on the federal arsenal at Springfield.
- The Confederation Congress had **no standing army and no funds** to respond; Massachusetts was forced to rely on a privately funded state militia to suppress the uprising.
- News of the rebellion alarmed nationalist leaders across the states, including George Washington, who wrote urgently to colleagues that the Confederation was on the verge of collapse into anarchy.

### Key Insight for Week 4 Discussion

Shays's Rebellion functioned as the psychological tipping point: it transformed abstract concerns about the Confederation's fiscal and commercial weaknesses into a vivid, immediate fear of social and political disorder — making the case for a stronger national government concrete and urgent rather than theoretical.

---

## Section 4: Congress Authorizes the Philadelphia Convention

### Core Concept: A Limited Mandate, Soon Exceeded

- In February 1787, the Confederation Congress formally authorized a convention of state delegates to meet in Philadelphia "for the sole and express purpose of revising the Articles of Confederation" (McClellan, pp. 238–240).
- This limited, revision-only mandate is historically significant: the delegates who gathered in May 1787 would ultimately **discard the Articles entirely** and draft a wholly new frame of government — a decision of highly contested legitimacy, since it exceeded their formal instructions.
- McClellan notes that this tension (limited mandate vs. the delegates' expansive action) would become a recurring point of criticism from Antifederalists during the ratification debates.

### Key Insight for Week 4 Discussion

The gap between the Convention's narrow legal mandate and its sweeping actual output raises an enduring constitutional question directly relevant to federalism: when circumstances demand it, do constituent assemblies possess an inherent authority to exceed their formal instructions in service of the people's fundamental interests? This question echoes through later debates about implied powers and "living constitutionalism."

## Capstone Connection

The chain of escalating crises McClellan documents here — from a narrow bilateral dispute (Mount Vernon) to a failed multilateral meeting (Annapolis) to a full-blown legitimacy crisis (Shays's Rebellion) — offers a useful analytical template for tracking how incremental coordination failures in decentralized health systems can accumulate into full governance crises. In emerging economies, a failure to resolve small interjurisdictional data-sharing disputes (the "Mount Vernon" scale) can cascade into system-wide interoperability breakdowns that eventually demand centralized intervention (the "Philadelphia" scale) — but, per McClellan's account, such intervention is legitimate only if it remains tethered to the consent and genuine needs of the constituent units, not imposed unilaterally from above.
