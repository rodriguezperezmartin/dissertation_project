# James McClellan, *Liberty, Order, and Justice* — Imperial Roots of American Federalism (pp. 289–297)

**Citation (APA 7):** McClellan, J. (2000). *Liberty, order, and justice: An introduction to the constitutional principles of American government* (3rd ed.). Liberty Fund.
**Pages:** 289–297
**Relevance:** Week 4 — Traces American federalism's intellectual origins back to pre-Revolutionary debates over the constitutional structure of the British Empire itself.

---

## Executive Summary

This section argues that American federalism was not invented in Philadelphia in 1787 — it was **discovered** by the colonists decades earlier, during their constitutional disputes with Parliament. McClellan traces how colonial leaders developed a theory of the British Empire as a *federal* structure: a collection of co-equal political communities (the colonial legislatures and the English Parliament) united under a common Crown, each possessing exclusive authority over its own internal affairs. This "dominion theory" or "commonwealth theory" of empire — developed by figures like James Wilson, John Adams, and Thomas Jefferson in the 1760s–1770s — directly supplied the conceptual vocabulary that the framers would later use to describe the relationship between the states and the new national government.

---

## Section 1: The Problem of Parliamentary Sovereignty

### Core Concept: Who Governs the Colonies?

- Prior to the 1760s, the British Empire operated under a largely unspoken and inconsistent constitutional arrangement — commonly described by historians as a period of **"salutary neglect,"** in which colonial assemblies exercised broad practical self-governance while nominally subordinate to Parliament (McClellan, pp. 289–291).
- After the Seven Years' War (French and Indian War), Britain's effort to assert direct parliamentary taxation (Sugar Act 1764, Stamp Act 1765, Townshend Acts 1767) forced colonists to articulate, for the first time, a coherent theory of *where* ultimate governing authority over the colonies actually resided.
- The orthodox British position held that Parliament possessed **supreme and indivisible sovereignty** over the entire Empire, including the colonies — a single, unified sovereign body could not logically be divided.

### Key Insight for Week 4 Discussion

The imperial crisis forced the colonists to confront a question that would resurface, in a different form, at the center of the American federal system: can sovereignty be legitimately *divided* between distinct levels of government, or must ultimate authority always rest in a single, final seat of power?

---

## Section 2: The Colonial "Dominion" or "Commonwealth" Theory

### Core Concept: A Federal Empire of Co-Equal Legislatures

- Colonial pamphleteers and legislators — including **James Wilson** (*Considerations on the Nature and Extent of the Legislative Authority of the British Parliament*, 1774), **John Adams**, and **Thomas Jefferson** (*A Summary View of the Rights of British America*, 1774) — advanced a competing theory: the British Empire was not a single sovereign state, but a **federation of equal, self-governing political communities united only by their shared allegiance to the Crown** (McClellan, pp. 291–294).
- Under this view, Parliament's authority was legitimate *only within Great Britain itself*; it held no inherent authority to legislate for the internal affairs of the colonies, whose own assemblies were the sole legitimate legislatures for colonial matters (particularly taxation).
- This is the theoretical basis for the famous colonial formula: **"no taxation without representation."** The colonists were not demanding seats in Parliament — they were denying Parliament's jurisdiction over their internal affairs altogether, just as Parliament could not tax Hanover or Scotland's internal affairs through a foreign legislature.
- The only link holding the empire together, on this theory, was the person of the King — making the relationship among Britain, Virginia, Massachusetts, and the other dominions **essentially federal**, each sharing a common executive (the Crown) while retaining separate, equal, and independent legislatures.

### Key Insight for Week 4 Discussion

This is the direct conceptual ancestor of the Confederation's "league of friendship" structure and, later, of the federal design of the Constitution: multiple co-equal political communities, independently sovereign over their own internal affairs, linked by a shared external frame (first the Crown, later the Articles, then the Constitution).

---

## Section 3: From Imperial Theory to Constitutional Practice

### Core Concept: Declaration of Independence as the Federal Empire's Dissolution and Rebirth

- McClellan argues that the **Declaration of Independence** itself should be read as the final move in this imperial-federal argument: having concluded that the British Empire's federal compact had been broken by the King's abuses (not merely Parliament's, since the colonists had already denied Parliament's jurisdiction), the colonies declared themselves "Free and Independent States" — now federating with *each other* instead of with Britain (McClellan, pp. 294–297).
- The language of the Declaration — "the unanimous Declaration of the thirteen united States of America" — preserves this federal self-understanding: 13 separate political communities acting in concert, not a single new unified nation suddenly springing into existence.
- This imperial-federal thinking explains why the Articles of Confederation (and later the Constitution) so naturally adopted a structure of divided, co-existing sovereignties — the colonists had spent a decade rehearsing exactly this argument against Parliament, and simply redirected the same federal logic toward their new domestic union.

### Key Insight for Week 4 Discussion

American federalism is best understood not as an invention of 1787, but as the domestic application of an imperial constitutional theory the colonists had already developed and tested in their decade-long dispute with Britain. This is precisely the argument Dr. Ross makes in his Week 4 video: "the beginning, America was Federal" — and it was federal first as a matter of colonial constitutional theory, long before it was federal as a matter of written constitutional text.

## Comparative Note: Imperial Federalism vs. Constitutional Federalism

| Dimension | Pre-1776 Imperial Federalism | Post-1787 Constitutional Federalism |
|---|---|---|
| **Unifying link** | The Crown (shared monarch) | The Constitution (shared written compact) |
| **Constituent units** | Colonial legislatures (Virginia, Massachusetts, etc.) | State governments |
| **Central claim of authority** | Parliament (rejected by colonists) | Congress (accepted, but limited/enumerated) |
| **Division of authority** | Internal colonial affairs vs. matters of empire-wide concern (war, trade — contested) | Enumerated federal powers vs. reserved state powers (10th Amendment) |
| **Theoretical basis** | Dominion/Commonwealth theory (Wilson, Adams, Jefferson) | Compact theory / popular sovereignty ("We the People") |

## Capstone Connection

McClellan's account of the colonists arguing for *jurisdictional co-equality* rather than mere representation (they did not simply want seats in Parliament — they denied Parliament's jurisdiction entirely) offers a sharp conceptual tool for your capstone research. When national ministries of health in emerging economies attempt to impose centralized digital infrastructure mandates on regional or departmental health authorities, the relevant constitutional/administrative question is analogous: does the central authority possess legitimate jurisdiction over the *internal implementation details* of local health systems, or only over matters of genuinely national concern (interoperability standards, cross-border health security)? The "dominion theory" distinction between internal self-governance and external/national-level coordination directly maps onto debates over appropriate centralization in health IT governance.
