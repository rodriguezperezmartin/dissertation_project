# David C. Hendrickson, *Peace Pact: The Lost World of the American Founding*

**Citation (APA 7):** Hendrickson, D. C. (2003). *Peace pact: The lost world of the American founding*. University Press of Kansas.
**Pages:** Assigned as general/background reading (no specific page range provided)
**Relevance:** Week 4 — A revisionist interpretation of the American founding that reframes the Articles of Confederation and the Constitution as fundamentally instruments of *interstate peace* rather than purely domestic constitutional documents.

---

## Executive Summary

Hendrickson's central thesis is that the American founding is best understood not primarily through the lens of domestic constitutional theory (how should *one* nation govern itself?) but through the lens of **international relations and peace-making among quasi-sovereign states** (how can *thirteen separate political communities* avoid the wars, rivalries, and power-balancing struggles that plagued the European state system?). On this reading, both the Articles of Confederation and the Constitution are best understood as **peace pacts** — treaty-like arrangements whose primary purpose was to prevent the states from treating each other as foreign rivals, as European states so often did. Hendrickson argues that this "lost world" of founding-era thought — in which the states were understood as essentially sovereign actors in a mini international system — has been obscured by later nationalist historiography that reads the Constitution purely as the founding of "one nation," rather than as a security compact among several.

---

## Section 1: The States as a "Mini International System"

### Core Concept: Union as Alternative to a European-Style Balance of Power

- Hendrickson argues that the Founders were deeply read in European diplomatic history and were acutely aware that a fractured North America — 13 (or more) separate sovereign states without a unifying compact — risked recreating the perpetual warfare, shifting alliances, and foreign intervention that characterized the European state system.
- Union, in this view, was not primarily about efficient domestic administration — it was a **security project**: a way of ensuring that the states would not go to war with one another, would not seek foreign alliances against each other, and would not fall prey to European powers exploiting their divisions (as Britain and France had long exploited rivalries among German or Italian states).
- This reading gives fresh meaning to Federalist No. 13's warning (highlighted in Dr. Ross's Week 4 video and Prof. Root's introduction) that disunion would have left the former colonies vulnerable to "being conquered by sections" by foreign powers.

### Key Insight for Week 4 Discussion

Hendrickson's framework reframes "federalism" itself: rather than asking only "how should power be divided between state and national governments?" his lens asks "how does this constitutional structure function as a peace treaty among potentially rival sovereignties?" This is a genuinely international-relations-inflected reading of a document usually treated as purely domestic constitutional law.

---

## Section 2: The Articles and Constitution as "Peace Pacts"

### Core Concept: Continuity Between 1781 and 1787

- Hendrickson pushes back against the common narrative that treats the Articles of Confederation (1781) and the Constitution (1789) as a sharp break — weak failure followed by strong success. Instead, he emphasizes **continuity of purpose**: both documents were fundamentally designed to keep the peace among the states and manage their relations with foreign powers.
- The Constitution strengthened the Union's institutional capacity (a real executive, a real judiciary, taxing power, commerce power) precisely because the Articles' security-and-peace-keeping function had proven too weak to prevent the kinds of interstate frictions (trade wars, boundary disputes, competing foreign alliances) that could, left unaddressed, escalate into the sort of conflict the Founders feared.
- Hendrickson is careful to note that this does not mean the states ceased to be meaningfully sovereign under the Constitution — rather, they pooled specific sovereign functions (war-making, foreign policy, interstate commerce) into a common federal government precisely to prevent competition among themselves on those dimensions, while retaining sovereignty over everything else.

### Key Insight for Week 4 Discussion

This "peace pact" thesis helps explain why the framers were so preoccupied with preventing states from forming separate alliances, conducting independent foreign policy, or making war on each other (see Constitution, Article I, Section 10) — concerns that make far more sense as *international security* provisions than as ordinary domestic administrative law.

---

## Section 3: The "Lost World" — Why This Reading Faded from View

### Core Concept: Nationalist Historiography Obscured the International Dimension

- Hendrickson argues that after the Civil War, American historiography increasingly told the founding story as the creation of a single, indivisible nation — a narrative that served the ideological needs of post-war nationalism (and the delegitimization of secession) but obscured the founding generation's own, more international and compact-based self-understanding.
- By recovering 18th-century diplomatic and international-law vocabulary (the language of leagues, confederacies, and pacts), Hendrickson tries to restore a "lost world" in which the states' quasi-sovereign status and their collective interest in avoiding a European-style balance-of-power system were central, explicit concerns of the framers — not merely background assumptions.

### Key Insight for Week 4 Discussion

This "lost world" argument connects directly to McClellan's "Imperial Roots" reading: both authors argue that modern readers tend to retroactively impose a nationalist or unitary framework onto a founding generation that actually understood its own project in far more federal, compact-based, and (per Hendrickson) international terms.

## Capstone Connection

Hendrickson's "peace pact" framework offers a distinctive analytical angle for your capstone: rather than viewing national digital health infrastructure mandates purely as administrative or technical policy, his lens invites you to ask whether such mandates also function as **coordination/peace mechanisms** among competing sub-national health authorities, donor organizations, or regional political factions. In Guatemala, for example, a decentralized digital health approach might be understood (through Hendrickson's lens) as deliberately avoiding the creation of a single powerful national health data authority that could become a locus of inter-departmental or inter-party rivalry — mirroring the Founders' caution about concentrating power in ways that could provoke internal conflict rather than cooperative peace.
