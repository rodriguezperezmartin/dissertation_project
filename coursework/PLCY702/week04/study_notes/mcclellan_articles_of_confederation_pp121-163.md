# James McClellan, *Liberty, Order, and Justice* — The Articles of Confederation (pp. 121–163)

**Citation (APA 7):** McClellan, J. (2000). *Liberty, order, and justice: An introduction to the constitutional principles of American government* (3rd ed.). Liberty Fund.
**Pages:** 121–163
**Relevance:** Week 4 — Origins, structure, and significance of America's first national constitution, the Articles of Confederation.

---

## Executive Summary

This section traces the Continental Congress's transition from a body of wartime resistance to the author of America's first constitutional charter. McClellan narrates the drafting of the Articles of Confederation (proposed 1776–1777, adopted by Congress November 1777, and finally ratified in 1781 after a four-year delay caused by disputes over western land claims). He emphasizes that the Articles were deliberately designed as a **"league of friendship"** among sovereign states rather than a consolidated national government — a structural choice that reflected the colonists' deep suspicion of centralized power, carried forward directly from their resistance to Parliament. McClellan then walks through the practical operation and eventual failures of this system, setting up the "crisis of the 1780s" that would lead to Philadelphia.

---

## Section 1: From Congress of Resistance to Congress of Government

### Core Concept: Continuity, Not Rupture

- The Continental Congress did not spring into being as a blank-slate government. It evolved out of the **inter-colonial committees of correspondence** and the First/Second Continental Congresses, bodies that had coordinated resistance to British taxation (McClellan, pp. 121–130).
- Because independence (July 1776) left the 13 states as separate sovereign entities with no overarching legal framework, Congress recognized the urgent need for a written compact to coordinate the war effort, foreign diplomacy, and interstate relations.
- John Dickinson authored the first full draft of the Articles in the summer of 1776 — significantly, this draft proposed a *stronger* national government than what was ultimately adopted. State jealousies over sovereignty, taxation authority, and western land claims forced extensive revisions (McClellan, pp. 130–138).

### Key Insight for Week 4 Discussion

The Articles were not an experiment in utopian political theory — they were a **pragmatic compromise** among 13 jealously independent sovereignties that had just rejected centralized imperial authority and had no intention of re-creating it domestically.

## Section 2: Structure of the Confederation Government

### Core Concept: A "Firm League of Friendship," Not a National Government

**Key institutional features (McClellan, pp. 138–150):**
- **Article II** explicitly declared: "Each state retains its sovereignty, freedom, and independence, and every power, jurisdiction, and right which is not by this Confederation expressly delegated to the United States, in Congress assembled."
- **Unicameral Congress:** A single-chamber Congress with **one vote per state** regardless of population — reflecting the compact-among-equals theory of union, not a nationalist theory of proportional representation.
- **Supermajority requirements:** Major legislative acts (declaring war, coining money, borrowing on the credit of the United States) required the assent of **9 of 13 states**.
- **Unanimity for amendment:** Any change to the Articles themselves required the consent of **all 13 state legislatures** — a provision that would prove fatal to reform efforts in the 1780s.
- **No separate executive branch** and **no national judiciary** — Congress itself executed its own laws through committees, and disputes between states were arbitrated through ad hoc congressional tribunals.
- **No power of direct taxation** — Congress could only requisition funds from the states, which frequently ignored or only partially met these requisitions.
- **No power to regulate interstate or foreign commerce** — leaving states free to erect tariffs against each other's goods, undermining a unified economic policy.

### Key Insight for Week 4 Discussion

The Articles' design directly mirrors the "federal" self-understanding the colonists had already developed under the British Empire (see Dr. Ross's Week 4 video and the "Imperial Roots" reading): co-equal political communities linked by a common compact, not subordinated to a central sovereign.

## Section 3: Achievements and Limitations of the Confederation

### What Worked (McClellan, pp. 150–157)

- The Confederation Congress successfully **concluded the Revolutionary War** diplomatically, securing the Treaty of Paris (1783) and prior recognition/alliance with France (1778).
- It passed the **Northwest Ordinance (1787)**, arguably the Confederation's most significant and enduring legislative achievement — establishing an orderly process for admitting new states on equal footing with the original 13, and banning slavery in the Northwest Territory.
- It demonstrated that a purely confederal structure *could* coordinate collective defense and diplomacy among sovereign states, at least under conditions of existential external threat (the war).

### What Failed (McClellan, pp. 157–163)

- **Fiscal insolvency:** Congress could not reliably fund the war debt or pay Continental soldiers, leading to near-mutinies (e.g., the Newburgh Conspiracy, 1783).
- **Commercial chaos:** States taxed each other's goods at the border, undermining a national market and provoking retaliatory trade wars among the states themselves.
- **Inability to enforce treaties:** Individual states ignored provisions of the Treaty of Paris (e.g., failing to honor debts owed to British creditors or restore Loyalist property), and Congress had no mechanism to compel compliance.
- **No means of self-amendment:** The unanimity requirement for amendments meant that even widely recognized defects (e.g., granting Congress power to tax) could be blocked by a single recalcitrant state (Rhode Island notoriously blocked the 1781 and 1783 impost amendments).

### Key Insight for Week 4 Discussion

McClellan frames the Confederation's failures not as evidence that federalism itself was flawed, but as evidence that **this particular degree of decentralization** — a government with no power to tax, regulate commerce, or enforce its own laws — was unsustainable for a union facing genuine security and economic challenges. This sets up the central Week 4 question: how much national power is *necessary* without destroying the "federal" character and local liberty the colonists fought to preserve?

## Comparative Note: Confederation Government vs. 1787 Constitution

| Feature | Articles of Confederation (1781) | U.S. Constitution (1789) |
|---|---|---|
| **Legislature** | Unicameral, 1 vote per state | Bicameral (House by population, Senate by state) |
| **Executive** | None (committees) | Single President |
| **Judiciary** | None (ad hoc arbitration) | Permanent federal courts |
| **Taxation** | Requisitions only (no direct power) | Direct taxing power |
| **Commerce** | No federal regulation | Federal commerce power |
| **Amendment** | Unanimous consent of states | 3/4 of states (via Congress or convention) |
| **Sovereignty theory** | "League of friendship" among sovereign states | "We the People" — compound/mixed sovereignty |

## Capstone Connection

McClellan's account of the Confederation's structural inability to enforce uniform policy across jealous, semi-sovereign states is directly analogous to the coordination failures that plague healthcare digital infrastructure efforts across emerging economies' sub-national jurisdictions (states, provinces, departamentos). Just as the Confederation Congress could request but not compel state compliance with federal policy, national ministries of health in decentralized emerging democracies (e.g., Guatemala's departmental health authorities) often *request* compliance with interoperability standards or data-sharing protocols but lack enforcement mechanisms — producing the same patchwork, uneven implementation McClellan describes among the 13 states in the 1780s.
