# Dr. Jason Ross — Week 4 Video: Federalism and American Constitutionalism

**Citation (APA 7):** Ross, J. (2026). *Federalism and American constitutionalism* [Video lecture transcript]. PLCY 702, Liberty University.
**Relevance:** Week 4 — Introduces federalism as a constitutive (not merely structural) feature of American political life, tracing its origins to the distinct colonial foundings and the colonists' developing theory of the British Empire as federal.

---

## Executive Summary

Dr. Ross argues that federalism is not simply a design choice written into the 1787 Constitution — it is a **constitutive fact of the American political experience**, present from the very first colonial settlements. Because England's North American colonies were founded at different times, under different charters, by different groups with different religious and political characters, America was federal "in the beginning." Ross traces how this patchwork of distinct colonial origins nonetheless shared a common English legal and political heritage (legislative assemblies, legal rights), which the colonists later used to argue — against Parliament — that they were co-equal, self-governing parts of a federal British Empire. He then follows the thread from the Declaration of Independence through to Tocqueville's observations on the township, and closes with a provocative question about whether the "spirit of liberty" still has a meaningful home in American local government today.

---

## Section 1: Distinct Colonial Origins

### Core Concept: Federalism Preceded the Constitution — "In the Beginning, America Was Federal"

- **Virginia (1607):** Founded by the Virginia Company under a royal charter — a commercial venture authorized directly by the Crown (Ross, Week 4 Video Script).
- **Plymouth (1620):** Founded by Pilgrims who had intended to sail to Virginia but went off course, landing outside the reach of any royal charter. They responded by drafting the **Mayflower Compact**, voluntarily agreeing "to covenant and combine themselves together into a civil body politic."
- **Massachusetts Bay (c. 1630):** Established about a decade later by the Puritans, who, despite settling nearby, had a markedly different political and religious character from the Pilgrims of Plymouth.
- **Pennsylvania (1681):** Founded via a **proprietary charter** granted to William Penn, a devout Quaker, who established a government notably more democratic than most of its contemporaries.

### Key Insight for Week 4 Discussion

Each colony had its own specific origin, its own founders, its own relationship to the British Empire, and ultimately its own distinct political and cultural history. Federalism, in Ross's framing, was not a 1787 invention layered onto a previously unified people — it reflects genuine, pre-existing political and cultural diversity among 13 separate founding communities.

---

## Section 2: Shared English Political Inheritance

### Core Concept: Common Legal Culture Eased Eventual Union

- Despite their differences, Britain's North American colonies shared certain general political institutions and assumptions inherited from English politics:
  - The belief that their power to settle and hold land in North America was ultimately granted by the king.
  - The belief that they should be governed by laws established through a legislative assembly (with varying degrees of democratic character).
  - The establishment of legal protections or rights for members of their political communities.
- This **common legal culture and heritage**, Ross argues, is what later eased the process of uniting these different and distinct colonies into a single federal union.

### Key Insight for Week 4 Discussion

Shared constitutional vocabulary (legislative assemblies, legal rights, consent-based governance) functioned as a kind of common language that allowed 13 culturally distinct colonies to eventually recognize each other as political equals capable of federating — directly paralleling McClellan's "Imperial Roots" reading.

---

## Section 3: The Federal British Empire and the Road to Independence

### Core Concept: Colonists Saw Themselves as Co-Equal Parts of a Federal Empire

- After Britain's victory in the French and Indian War, the Empire moved away from a prior period of "benign neglect" and began asserting direct control and taxation over the colonies to offset war debt.
- American colonial leaders argued that a core principle of English constitutionalism was **the right not to be taxed without consent**: "We have our own legislative assemblies," they argued. "If we are to be taxed by the British Empire, we should consent to these taxes through our own assemblies" — not through a Parliament in which they had no representation.
- Crucially, Ross notes the colonists did not see themselves as *disconnected* from England — rather, they saw themselves as **co-equal parts of a federal British Empire**, with their own legislative assemblies representing them just as Parliament represented the English, under a shared King. "The people of Virginia or Massachusetts should not be subject to laws written by the people of England any more than the people of England would subject themselves to laws written by the people of Virginia or Massachusetts" (Ross, Week 4 Video Script).
- The **Declaration of Independence** itself asserts this federal theory of empire and transfers it into the new Union, presenting itself as "the unanimous declaration of the 13 united States of America." States were jealous of their own sovereignty and, as Ross notes, would remain reluctant to surrender that sovereignty to a new central government.

### Key Insight for Week 4 Discussion

This section directly corresponds to McClellan's "Imperial Roots of American Federalism" reading — Ross's accessible narrative account and McClellan's more technical constitutional-history account describe the exact same intellectual development: the colonists' "dominion theory" of a federal empire.

## Section 4: Defining American Federalism and Connecting to Tocqueville

### Core Concept: Two Levels of Government, and the Deeper Township Connection

- **Strict definition:** American federalism refers to a political system in which the national government exercises certain constitutionally enumerated powers over the nation's entire territory, while state governments retain and exercise powers not specifically granted to the national government.
- Ross distinguishes this from **subsidiarity** — the broader principle that political and social problems should be addressed at the lowest possible level of a hierarchy of institutions. Federalism, strictly speaking, addresses only two levels (national and state), but Ross highlights Tocqueville's effort to trace liberty further down — through the state, through the county, into the **township** as the "primal source of liberty."
- Ross quotes Tocqueville directly: *"The strength of free peoples resides in the township. Township institutions are to liberty what primary schools are to knowledge. They put it within the grasp of the people. They give them a taste of its peaceful practice and accustom them to its use. Without town institutions, a nation can pretend to have a free government, but does not possess the spirit of liberty."*

### Key Insight for Week 4 Discussion

Ross draws attention to the common conflation of federalism with "states' rights" — a framing that was powerful before the Civil War but attenuated after the 14th Amendment. He argues Tocqueville's deeper point — that liberty is *practiced* at the township level — remains the more enduring and analytically useful insight, connecting Week 3's township material directly to Week 4's federalism focus.

## Section 5: Modern Relevance — States as "Laboratories of Democracy"

### Core Concept: Is There Still a Living Space for Local Liberty?

- Ross notes that while pre–Civil War claims of state sovereignty have largely been overcome as a legal matter, many scholars argue for the continuing relevance of states as **"laboratories of democracy"** — proposing policy innovations that could not be attempted at the federal level.
- He observes an **ideological sorting** occurring in American politics following COVID-19, in which state legislatures and governors increasingly diverge in policy approach — a trend that may, over the next generation, make states more powerful relative to the federal government as the country enters "this period of political transformation."
- Ross closes with a pointed, open question for students: in a politics that is strictly national, can the rejuvenation of state governments be connected with the re-emergence of spaces where individuals can exercise their liberty and come to embrace it as something real? **"Or does liberty now exist only as an ideological abstraction that we debate about?"**

### Key Insight for Week 4 Discussion

This closing question is an explicit prompt for the Week 4 discussion thread and your Annotated Bibliography research interest: is American federalism today a living, practiced reality (per Tocqueville's township ideal) or has it become a merely rhetorical/ideological battleground detached from genuine local self-governance?

## Comparative Note: Federalism vs. Subsidiarity vs. States' Rights

| Concept | Core Claim | Levels Addressed |
|---|---|---|
| **Federalism (strict)** | National government has enumerated powers; states retain the rest | National + State (2 levels) |
| **Subsidiarity** | Problems should be solved at the lowest effective level | Any level in a hierarchy (national → state → county → township → individual) |
| **States' Rights (historical usage)** | Emphasizes state sovereignty claims against federal power | Primarily National vs. State, often pre–Civil War framing |
| **Tocqueville's Township Thesis** | Liberty is *practiced*, not just legally allocated, at the most local level | Township (the base of the hierarchy) |
