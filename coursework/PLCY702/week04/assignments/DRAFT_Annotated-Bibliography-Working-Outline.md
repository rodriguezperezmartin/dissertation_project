### 1. Alvarado, Lainfiesta, Paniagua-Avila, & Asturias (2022)

**Citation:** Alvarado, J. R., Lainfiesta, X., Paniagua-Avila, A., & Asturias, G. (2022). Developing a digital technology system to address COVID-19 health needs in Guatemala: A scientific diaspora case study. *Frontiers in Research Metrics and Analytics, 7*, 899611. https://doi.org/10.3389/frma.2022.899611

**Outline — draft in your own words:**
- *Thesis:* Guatemalan diaspora professionals + a domestic nonprofit (FUNDEGUA) self-organized to build an AI chatbot/call-center system (ALMA) to fight COVID misinformation, operating alongside — not replacing — government health authority.
- *Method:* Descriptive case study of the initiative's founding principles, partnership structure, and team composition.
- *Key findings:* Success depended on Ministry of Health endorsement (legitimizing authority), nonprofit legal/fundraising scaffolding, and diaspora volunteer labor — a hybrid of private/voluntary action operating under public-sector permission.
- *Constitutional angle to develop:* Frame this as evidence of **subsidiarity/local-nonprofit initiative filling gaps left by centralized state capacity** — connect to the Articles of Confederation's lesson that weak central resourcing pushes real implementation capacity down to local/voluntary actors, and to Tocqueville's observations on civic/associational life substituting for centralized administration.

### 2. Baez, Acra, & Difo (2026)

**Citation:** Baez, A. A., Acra, C., & Difo, B. (2026). Adoption, perceived value, and barriers to generative artificial intelligence in emergency medicine: A cross-sectional survey of Latin American clinicians. *International Journal of Critical Illness and Injury Science, 16*(3), 92–99. https://doi.org/10.4103/ijciis.ijciis_43_26

**Outline — draft in your own words:**
- *Thesis:* Examines how Latin American emergency medicine clinicians perceive and adopt generative AI tools, and what barriers limit adoption.
- *Method:* Cross-sectional survey of 123 clinicians across Latin America.
- *Key findings:* Clinicians see value in GenAI but cite infrastructure, training, and regulatory/trust barriers to adoption.
- *Constitutional angle to develop:* Barriers reported (regulatory ambiguity, institutional trust) map onto questions of **administrative authority and due process in regulating emerging medical technology** — tie to the research question's interest in how constitutional limits on administrative power shape (or stall) digital-health implementation at the point of clinical practice.

### 3. Béland, Marchildon, Medrano, & Rocco (2024)

**Citation:** Béland, D., Marchildon, G. P., Medrano, A., & Rocco, P. (2024). Policy feedback, varieties of federalism, and the politics of health-care funding in the United States, Mexico, and Canada. *Politics & Policy, 52*(1), 51–69. https://doi.org/10.1111/polp.12575

**Outline — draft in your own words:**
- *Thesis:* Compares how different federalist structures (U.S., Mexico, Canada) shape health-care funding politics and policy feedback over time.
- *Method:* Comparative historical/institutional policy analysis across three federalist systems.
- *Key findings:* Varieties of federalism (centralized vs. decentralized funding authority) produce path-dependent differences in how health-funding policy evolves and who holds blame/credit.
- *Constitutional angle to develop:* This is your **strongest direct comparative-federalism source** — use it to explicitly connect U.S. constitutional federalism (this week's topic) to Mexico's federalist structure, directly supporting a comparative research question about constitutional limits on central authority shaping health policy outcomes.

### 4. Fabre & Straub (2023)

**Citation:** Fabre, A., & Straub, S. (2023). The impact of public–private partnerships (PPPs) in infrastructure, health, and education. *Journal of Economic Literature, 61*(2), 655–715. https://doi.org/10.1257/jel.20211607

**Outline — draft in your own words:**
- *Thesis:* Comprehensive literature review assessing whether PPPs in infrastructure/health/education deliver on their efficiency promises.
- *Method:* Systematic review of empirical PPP literature across sectors and countries (heavy Latin American renegotiation evidence — Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Peru, Mexico).
- *Key findings:* Health PPPs "mostly display disappointing results" due to misaligned incentives and undiagnosed demand-side barriers; renegotiation is common and often favors private partners.
- *Constitutional angle to develop:* PPP renegotiation dynamics raise **property rights, contract enforcement, and due process questions** about how constitutional protections (or their absence) shape the government's bargaining power with private partners in emerging-economy health infrastructure deals.

### 7. Leite, de Vasconcelos, & Costa Lima (2011)

**Citation:** Leite, V. R., de Vasconcelos, C. M., & Costa Lima, K. (2011). Federalism and decentralization: Impact on international and Brazilian health policies. *International Journal of Health Services, 41*(4), 711–723. https://doi.org/10.2190/HS.41.4.f

**Outline — draft in your own words:**
- *Thesis:* No single model of health-system federalism exists; Brazil's decentralized system increasingly resembles U.S.-style federalism but is undermined by fiscal inequality among subnational units.
- *Method:* Theoretical/comparative review using OECD, WHO, PAHO, and Brazilian government financing data.
- *Key findings:* Successful decentralization requires stable funding, trained local managers, **improved information systems**, and defined cooperation patterns between federal levels.
- *Constitutional angle to develop:* This is your most **direct federalism-theory bridge** — explicitly draws the U.S.–Brazil federalism comparison, and its call for "improved information systems" as a precondition for functioning decentralization connects classic federalism theory straight to your digital-health-infrastructure research question.
