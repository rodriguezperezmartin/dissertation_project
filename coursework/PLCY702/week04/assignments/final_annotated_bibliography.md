## Research Interest

Emerging economies, such as Guatemala, struggle to deliver equitable healthcare due to financial, educational, and technical barriers, often relying on fragmented nonprofit interventions (Alvarado et al., 2022) while attempting to adapt models from developed nations. Mexico exemplifies this adaptation, having recently looked to United States public policies regarding healthcare interoperability. Despite these barriers, emerging economies possess a distinct structural advantage: the absence of legacy information technology systems allows them to leapfrog directly to artificial intelligence (AI) and digital health platforms. Achieving this outcome requires a constitutional balance between centralized public policy and local public and private institutions. Central governments can establish policy frameworks and market incentives, while local institutions organize and operationalize the enterprises necessary to deliver equitable care --- reflecting McClellan's (2000) analysis of balancing central coordination with decentralized power and Tocqueville's (1835/2012) emphasis on local institutions. This capstone research, conducted within the Doctor of Public Policy program's Economic Policy cognate, examines how effective public policies strengthen a nation's human capital and reduce reactive healthcare budget expenditures. The central research question guiding this inquiry is: How do constitutional divisions of authority between central governments and local institutions (both public and private) shape the effectiveness of incentive-based public policies designed to drive private-sector AI and digital health adoption in emerging economies?

## Annotated Bibliography

Alvarado, J. R., Lainfiesta, X., Paniagua-Avila, A., & Asturias, G. (2022). Developing a digital technology system to address COVID-19 health needs in Guatemala: A scientific diaspora case study. *Frontiers in Research Metrics and Analytics*, *7*, Article 899611. <https://doi.org/10.3389/frma.2022.899611>

> Alvarado et al. (2022) examines in this article how non-state actors -- specifically Guatemalan scientists living abroad and a local nonprofit group called FUNDEGUA -- built an artificial intelligence (AI) powered chatbot alongside a call-center system (ALMA). The objective was to combat COVID-19 misinformation and to expand healthcare navigation. This initiative operated alongside national health authorities and was not put in place to displace national systems. The authors support these arguments through a descriptive case study that analyzes ALMA's founding principles, organizational governance, cross-sector partnerships, and the multidisciplinary team construction. Their work demonstrates that operations success relies on a three-part hybrid model which combines endorsements from Guatemala's Ministry of Health, legal and fundraising scaffolding from FUNDEGUA, and technical labor supplies from diaspora volunteers. This model provides a clear example of how private and voluntary actors have the capacity to deliver digital health tools under the oversight and supervision of a centralized governing authority. ALMA offers a real-world example of subsidiarity and Tocqueville's (1835/2012) observations on civic associations stepping in where centralized administration bodies fall short, mirroring the Articles of Confederation lesson (McClellan, 2000) that weak central resourcing pushes practical implementation down to local and voluntary individuals. This evidence answers the research question by showing how divisions of authority between central governments and local public and private institutions shape Artificial Intelligence and digital health adoption in emerging economies.

Baez, A. A., Acra, C., & Difo, B. (2026). Adoption, perceived value, and barriers to generative artificial intelligence in emergency medicine: A cross-sectional survey of Latin American clinicians. *International Journal of Critical Illness and Injury Science*, *16*(3), 92--99. <https://doi.org/10.4103/ijciis.ijciis_43_26>

> Baez et al. (2026) examines how Latin American emergency medicine clinicians are more readily adopting generative artificial intelligence (AI) healthcare tools and applications, alongside the obstacles that restrict more frequent clinical usage. The authors in their research gather evidence on these trends through a cross-sectional survey of 123 emergency medicine clinicians spanning Latin America. The survey measured current adoption patterns, perceived value at the clinical level, and barriers to workflow integration. Survey findings demonstrated that while clinicians recognize substantial practical value in generative AI for emergency care, consistent adoption remains blocked by limited understanding of governance, infrastructure deficits, insufficient structured training, regulatory restrictions, and low institutional trust. Where regulatory ambiguity and low institutional trust persist, critical constitutional questions emerge regarding administrative authority, the rule of law, and due process in overseeing emerging medical technologies. As central governments fail to establish clear, constitutionally bounded regulatory frameworks, this ambiguity creates an environment of uncertainty that stalls implementation at the local clinical level. Without predictable legal guardrails from central authorities, local hospital and private innovators do not have the institutional confidence needed to operationalize digital health platforms at scale. This is even a hurdle in large healthcare organizations here in America. This study provides empirical evidence which illustrates how constitutional divisions of authority between central governments and local public and private institutions shape the effectiveness of incentive-based public policies designed to drive private-sector AI and digital health adoption in emerging economies.

Béland, D., Marchildon, G. P., Medrano, A., & Rocco, P. (2024). Policy feedback, varieties of federalism, and the politics of health-care funding in the United States, Mexico, and Canada. *Politics & Policy*, *52*(1), 51--69. <https://doi.org/10.1111/polp.12575>

> Béland et al. (2024) in their research examines how distinct varieties of federalism across the United States, Mexico, and Canada shape the politics of healthcare funding and long-term policy feedback. The authors employ a comparative historical and institutional policy analysis across these three federalist nations. They trace how constitutional funding rules and intergovernmental arrangements have evolved over time. Their findings show that centralizing or decentralizing constitutional funding power creates lasting differences in how national healthcare policies develop across regions. The comparative evidence reveals how differing fiscal structures influence the way political representatives allocate credit or blame. It also reveals how durable healthcare reforms become across subnational units. By bridging United States constitutional federalism with Mexico's federalist system, this study reinforces core constitutional principles regarding the division of authority between central and subnational governments, echoing McClellan's (2000) analysis of balancing central coordination with decentralized power. The authors also illustrate that constitutional boundaries governing fiscal and administrative power dictates which policy instruments gain institutional traction, which encounter intergovernmental friction, and how subnational entities respond to national mandates. This comparative analysis directly informs the research question by demonstrating how constitutional divisions of authority between central governments and local public and private institutions shape the effectiveness of incentive-based public policies designed to drive private-sector artificial intelligence (AI) and digital health adoption in emerging economies.

Fabre, A., & Straub, S. (2023). The impact of public--private partnerships (PPPs) in infrastructure, health, and education. *Journal of Economic Literature*, *61*(2), 655--715. <https://doi.org/10.1257/jel.20211607>

> Fabre and Straub (2023) provide an economic review of public--private partnerships (PPPs). They offer a deeper look at their ability to deliver on promises of greater efficiency and improved service across three major sectors: infrastructure, health, and education. The article examines the contractual requirements and institutional conditions that foster either success or failure. To generate these insights, the authors conducted a comprehensive systematic review of empirical and theoretical PPP literature. Their research spanned across countries, drawing heavily on contract-renegotiation evidence from Latin America, including Mexico, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, and Peru. Their analysis yields two central findings: health-sector PPPs largely produce disappointing outcomes due to misaligned incentives, difficult quality assessment, and undiagnosed demand-side barriers, and ongoing contract renegotiations frequently favor private firms. This evidence demonstrates that public--private relationships require strong state oversight and reliable contract enforcement instead of serving as substitutes for weak institutional capacity. Without strong constitutional and rule-of-law guardrails---governing property rights, contract enforcement, and due process---private partners can capture public resources rather than expand equitable healthcare delivery. These components directly inform the research question as it illustrates how constitutional divisions of authority between central governments and local public and private institutions shape the effectiveness of incentive-based public policies designed to drive private-sector artificial intelligence (AI) and digital health adoption in emerging economies.

Forrer, J., Kee, J. E., Newcomer, K. E., & Boyer, E. (2010). Public--private partnerships and the public accountability question. *Public Administration Review*, *70*(3), 475--484. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6210.2010.02161.x>

> Forrer et al. (2010) examines the challenge of preserving public accountability when governments delegate infrastructure and service delivery to private enterprises via public--private partnerships (PPPs). To solve this governance gap, the authors created a model using existing research on public accountability. This model showed how public and private partners share decisions, financial risks, and daily oversight. Their analysis demonstrates that traditional hierarchical accountability mechanisms designed for purely public agencies fail to govern PPPs effectively. It prompted the authors to establish a six-dimension evaluation model encompassing risk allocation, costs and benefits, social and political impacts, technical expertise, partnership collaboration, and performance measurement. The framework revealed that engaging private partners does not diminish public-sector responsibility, shifting the state's role from direct service provider to contract manager and guarantor of public value. This accountability challenge directly engages constitutional limits on administrative power and democratic oversight. It raises essential questions regarding legal and political responsibility when public authorities delegate core healthcare functions to private technology firms. Without constitutionally grounded accountability structures, incentive-based partnerships risk blurring lines of authority and weakening institutional legitimacy. Forrer et al.'s (2010) multidimensional model informs the research question by providing a structured lens. This lens allows us to evaluate how constitutional divisions of authority between central governments and local public and private institutions shape incentive-based policies for private-sector artificial intelligence (AI) and digital health adoption in emerging economies.

Kula, N., & Fryatt, R. J. (2014). Public--private interactions on health in South Africa: Opportunities for scaling up. *Health Policy and Planning*, *29*(5), 560--569. <https://doi.org/10.1093/heapol/czt042>

> Kula and Fryatt (2014) examined public--private interactions within South Africa's healthcare sector to identify the institutional and policy conditions required to scale hybrid service delivery models responsibly. To evaluate these arrangements, the authors studied existing public-private partnerships (PPPs) in South Africa. They reviewed contract structures, government oversight, and results across clinical and infrastructure projects. Their analysis demonstrated that while private-sector engagement can expand clinical capacity and modernize service delivery in an emerging economy, scaling these initiatives demands rigorous evaluation mechanisms capable of detecting and correcting perverse incentives that arise when profit-driven agents perform public health functions. The authors indicate that without clear regulatory standards, transparent performance monitoring, and sustained public-sector management capacity, hybrid healthcare models risk fragmenting care and undermining health equity. Operating within South Africa's constitutional right-to-health framework, this case provides a valuable comparative anchor. It shows that constitutional accountability mechanisms must evolve alongside privatized and hybrid service delivery models. This institutional tension parallels United States federalism's ongoing constitutional debates regarding administrative authority, delegated governance, and the limits of public oversight over private contractors. Kula and Fryatt's (2014) findings directly inform the research question by illustrating how constitutional divisions of authority between central governments and local public and private institutions must incorporate enforceable accountability guardrails. This is crucial to ensure that incentive-based public policies drive equitable private-sector artificial intelligence (AI) and digital health adoption in emerging economies.

Leite, V. R., de Vasconcelos, C. M., & Costa Lima, K. (2011). Federalism and decentralization: Impact on international and Brazilian health policies. *International Journal of Health Services*, *41*(4), 711--723. <https://doi.org/10.2190/HS.41.4.f>

> Leite et al. (2011) examines the structural relationship between federalism, decentralization, and health policy, arguing that no single model of health-system federalism exists across nations. The authors draw on a theoretical and comparative review of international financing data from the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), the World Health Organization (WHO), the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO), and Brazilian government records. The authors analyze how Brazil's decentralized health system increasingly looks like the United States federalism while remaining constrained by severe fiscal differences among subnational units. Their comparative analysis demonstrates that constitutional decentralization alone does not guarantee equitable service delivery or administrative efficiency. The authors conclude that functional decentralized health systems require four institutional preconditions: 1 - stable intergovernmental funding, 2 - trained local managers, 3 - clearly defined patterns of federal--subnational cooperation, and 4- improved health information systems. By comparing Brazil and the United States, this study looks at how sharing funding and control between national and state or local governments affects health care delivery. Treating strong information systems as a must-have for decentralized governance connects traditional federalism directly to modern digital health infrastructure. Leite et al.'s (2011) findings directly inform the research question by illustrating how constitutional divisions of authority between central governments and local public and private institutions must pair fiscal coordination with technical infrastructure. This will ensure incentive-based artificial intelligence (AI) and digital health policies are effective in emerging economies.

Rotulo, A., Epstein, M., & Kondilis, E. (2020). Fiscal federalism vs fiscal decentralization in healthcare: A conceptual framework. *Hippokratia*, *24*(3), 107--113.

> Rotulo et al. (2020) established a conceptual distinction between fiscal federalism and fiscal decentralization, arguing that scholars and policymakers frequently blend these two models when analyzing healthcare financing authority. To clarify these institutional boundaries, the authors construct a theoretical framework. They synthesize public finance theory and comparative health-policy literature regarding revenue collection, intergovernmental transfers, and subnational spending discretion. Their analysis demonstrates that fiscal federalism represents a constitutional division of sovereign fiscal powers across autonomous tiers of government. On the other hand fiscal decentralization entails the administrative delegation of budgetary responsibilities from a central authority to subordinate regional or local entities. The authors indicate that these two structural arrangements produce noticeably different outcomes for health-system equity, allocative efficiency, and genuine local autonomy. This is because delegated authority remains vulnerable to central reversal while federalist powers carry institutional perpetuity. This conceptual distinction provides an essential definitional anchor for constitutional public policy research, directly illuminating the tension between centralized administration and local self-governance examined in McClellan's (2000) analysis of the Articles of Confederation and Tocqueville's (1835/2012) study of local institutions. Determining whether local healthcare authority is protected by a constitution or merely delegated by a central government helps guide comparative research across emerging economies like Mexico and Guatemala. Rotulo et al.'s (2020) framework directly supports this research question by offering a clear way to measure how power-sharing between central governments, local agencies, and private institutions shapes policies that incentivize private-sector AI and digital health adoption.

Venzor Strader, A., Castro Aragón, E., Coyote, E., Aguilar Ferro, A. I., & Rohloff, P. (2025). Integrating Indigenous Maya practices and digital health tools to improve outcomes for Indigenous newborns in Guatemala: A community-based initiative. *Global Health Action*, *18*(1), 2565870. <https://doi.org/10.1080/16549716.2025.2565870>

> Venzor Strader et al. (2025) examines a community-based nongovernmental initiative in Guatemala led by the Maya Health Alliance (Wuqu' Kawoq) that pairs traditional Maya midwives with a co-designed, offline smartphone application to improve neonatal health outcomes. Using program description and quantitative outcomes reporting---tracking monthly coverage, hospital referral rates, and neonatal mortality statistics from a community-health-worker-led intervention---the authors were able to evaluate how culturally grounded digital tools perform in resource-constrained rural settings. Their findings demonstrated substantial operational reach, with the program serving 85% of local newborns monthly and significantly increasing successful emergency referral rates. The authors also reveal a critical institutional barrier: discriminatory treatment at state-run referral hospitals frequently discourages Indigenous families from accepting formal medical care. This evidence raises urgent constitutional concerns regarding due process and equal protection, illustrating how centralized state health institutions can lose legitimacy when they fail to serve disregarded populations equitably. In response, locally autonomous, non-state delivery models fill the legitimacy and capacity gap left by central authorities, echoing Tocqueville's (1835/2012) emphasis on local institutions and the constitutional principle of subsidiarity. Venzor Strader et al.'s (2025) study directly informs the research question by demonstrating how constitutional divisions of authority between central governments and local public and private institutions shape whether incentive-based digital health and artificial intelligence (AI) policies achieve equitable adoption in emerging economies.

Yang, Y., Hou, Y., & Wang, Y. (2013). On the development of public--private partnerships in transitional economies: An explanatory framework. *Public Administration Review*, *73*(2), 301--310. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6210.2012.02672.x>

> Yang et al. (2013) investigates the institutional and economic determinants of public--private partnership (PPP) performance in transitional and emerging economies. They propose an explanatory framework to account for uneven implementation outcomes. To build this model, the authors conducted theoretical framework development grounded in comparative case evidence from transitional economies, organizing PPP dynamics around a three-pillar \"tripod\" structure. The tripod comprised of the market, the operating environment, and government capacity, alongside seven operational factors. Their analysis demonstrates that while market demand and technical feasibility matter, government credibility, enforceable institutional guarantees, and effective corruption control serve as the decisive factors determining whether private-sector partnerships succeed or fail. Without predictable public-sector commitments, private investors face prohibitive political and financial risks that discourage long-term infrastructure participation. These findings directly engage constitutional governance principles, as government credibility and institutional guarantees depend on constitutional constraints over administrative discretion and adherence to the rule of law. Establishing durable legal boundaries on central administrative power operates as a structural precondition for attracting private capital and technical innovation in emerging economies such as Guatemala and Mexico. Yang et al.'s (2013) framework directly informs the research question by illustrating how constitutional divisions of authority between central governments and local public and private institutions shape the effectiveness of incentive-based public policies designed to drive private-sector artificial intelligence (AI) and digital health adoption.
