## Source 1 — Rotulo, Epstein, & Kondilis (2020)

- **Citation (APA 7):** Rotulo, A., Epstein, M., & Kondilis, E. (2020). Fiscal federalism vs fiscal decentralization in healthcare: A conceptual framework. *Hippokratia, 24*(3), 107–113.
- **Source type:** Peer-reviewed journal article (narrative review)
- **Original source URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8256788/
- **Saved file:** `/Users/martinperezrodriguez/Downloads/Fiscal federalism vs fiscal decentralization in healthcare a conceptual framework.pdf`
- **Bucket fit:** **Bucket 1 (Federalism/decentralization + health policy)** — directly fills the biggest identified gap
- **Peer-review status confirmed:** Yes — published in *Hippokratia*, a peer-reviewed medical journal (Aristotle University of Thessaloniki / Queen Mary University of London authors)

### Structural Summary

**Central thesis/research question:** The article argues that "fiscal federalism" and "fiscal decentralization" are distinct health-financing policy options frequently conflated in the literature, with materially different and often opposing effects on healthcare equity, effectiveness, and efficiency. The paper provides conceptual clarification and maps each option's policy implications.

**Methodology/approach:** Narrative literature review (not systematic) of decentralization theory, following Greenhalgh et al.'s (2018) methodological framework. Literature identified via scoping search (PubMed, Web of Science) plus retrospective snowballing. Theoretical material is organized around three decentralization types — political, administrative, economic — with economic decentralization further split into the paper's two central constructs.

**Key findings:**
- **Fiscal federalism** = transfer of *spending authority only* over a centrally pooled health budget to local governments (pooling stays central; spending is local). Examples: UK, Cuba, Denmark, Brazil.
- **Fiscal decentralization** = transfer of *both pooling and spending* responsibility to local/regional authorities, fragmenting the national risk pool. Examples: Italy, Spain, China, Ivory Coast.
- Fiscal decentralization shows a correlation with reduced infant mortality in some econometric studies, but this benefit is conditional on regional wealth — poorer regions fare worse because fragmenting the pool destroys central cross-subsidization between high- and low-income areas, widening spatial health inequities.
- Fiscal federalism, when paired with adequate resources, equitable allocation formulas, and genuine local community participation in decision-making, is presented as the more favorable option for improving national health outcomes without sacrificing equity — but is "not a magic bullet": it fails under austerity, non-equitable allocation formulas, or exclusion of community input.
- Under neoliberal austerity conditions, the paper argues the distinction becomes moot — both paths tend toward commercialization/privatization outcomes.

**Relevance to the research question:** This source supplies the conceptual vocabulary needed to precisely classify "constitutional divisions of authority between central governments and local institutions" in health policy — the exact axis the research question investigates. It distinguishes *which* kind of authority division (spending-only vs. pooling-and-spending) is in play, a distinction neither the research paragraph nor prior sources currently make explicit. It also supplies a concrete mechanism (cross-subsidization loss under fragmented pooling) for *why* decentralization can undermine policy effectiveness and equity — directly relevant to evaluating whether incentive-based digital health adoption policies succeed or fail depending on the authority-division model in place. Country examples (Italy, Spain, China, Ivory Coast) also offer potential comparative data points for contextualizing Mexico/Guatemala.

**Potential annotation angle (for independent drafting):** Use this source to establish the conceptual distinction between fiscal federalism and fiscal decentralization as two different constitutional/administrative arrangements, then connect that distinction to the research question's interest in how authority division shapes policy effectiveness — noting the source's finding that fragmented local pooling (one form of decentralization) can undermine incentive-based policy goals by reducing resource adequacy in poorer regions, which has direct implications for equitable AI/digital health rollout.

**Gap check:** This source fills Bucket 1 (now 1 of 3–4 needed). Still need 2–3 more sources combining federalism/decentralization theory *specifically applied to health policy outcomes* — ideally with closer ties to Latin America or emerging economies specifically, since this source's country examples (UK, Cuba, Italy, Spain, China, Ivory Coast) do not include Mexico or Guatemala directly.

## Source 2 — Béland, Marchildon, Medrano, & Rocco (2024)

- **Citation (APA 7):** Béland, D., Marchildon, G. P., Medrano, A., & Rocco, P. (2024). Policy feedback, varieties of federalism, and the politics of health-care funding in the United States, Mexico, and Canada. *Politics & Policy, 52*(1), 51–69. https://doi.org/10.1111/polp.12575
- **Source type:** Peer-reviewed journal article (comparative historical-institutionalist analysis, process tracing)
- **Saved file:** `/Users/martinperezrodriguez/Downloads/Politics   Policy - 2024 - Béland - Policy feedback  varieties of federalism  and the politics of health‐care funding in.pdf`
- **Bucket fit:** **Bucket 1 (Federalism/decentralization + health policy)** — strongest source found so far, directly comparative with Mexico
- **Peer-review status confirmed:** Yes — *Politics & Policy*, Policy Studies Organization journal (Wiley)

### Structural Summary

**Central thesis/research question:** The article asks why the United States and Mexico experienced major intergovernmental changes to health-care funding arrangements before/during COVID-19, while Canada did not — despite all three being federal systems. It answers this by combining two concepts: **policy feedback** (how existing policy designs generate self-reinforcing or self-undermining political dynamics over time) and **"varieties of federalism"** (the idea that federalism is not a single binary category but a bundle of constitutional, fiscal, and institutional arrangements that differ by country and even by policy area).

**Methodology/approach:** Historical institutionalist, qualitative process-tracing comparative case study across three federal countries (US, Mexico, Canada), drawing on policy documents and existing scholarship rather than original quantitative data.

**Key findings:**
- **Mexico** is characterized as the *most centralized* of the three federations despite being constitutionally federal: fiscal authority is heavily concentrated at the national level, with states financially dependent on federal transfers. The 2004–2020 "Seguro Popular" (SP) program decentralized some *spending* authority to states (via state-level REPSS bodies) while keeping financing centralized — but poor state administrative capacity, weak democratic checks at the state level, and corruption in fund management produced a **self-undermining policy feedback loop** that justified the program's 2020 replacement by the more centralized INSABI under President AMLO.
- **United States** shows the opposite pattern: Medicaid's matching-fund design created a **self-reinforcing feedback loop** (states lobbying for more funds, political coalitions protecting/expanding the program), but its means-tested structure also produced **self-undermining** gaps (e.g., Oregon's failed eligibility expansion experiment), and the program remains subject to high partisan volatility (ACA expansion opt-outs, work-requirement litigation, post-pandemic "unwinding").
- **Canada** shows neither dynamic strongly — its Canada Health Transfer (block-grant, no strings attached) produced durable "path dependence" and a "paradigm freeze," insulating the system from major disruption even during COVID-19.
- **Core causal mechanism:** whether a federal health-financing arrangement is durable or prone to collapse/recentralization depends on *how* the original policy design distributes blame, discretion, and accountability between central and subnational governments — not simply on whether authority is centralized or decentralized in the abstract.

**Relevance to the research question:** This is the single strongest source identified so far for the capstone's framing. It directly operationalizes "constitutional divisions of authority between central governments and local institutions" using Mexico as one of its three cases, and demonstrates empirically how a specific policy design (Seguro Popular's decentralized spending/centralized financing split) failed due to weak subnational administrative capacity and accountability — closely paralleling the capstone's own interest in why Mexico's 2026 digital health mandate may encounter similar implementation resistance. The "policy feedback" framework also offers a mechanism (self-reinforcing vs. self-undermining dynamics) that could sharpen the capstone's theoretical model of why incentive-based policies succeed or fail across different authority-division arrangements.

**Potential annotation angle (for independent drafting):** Use this source's Mexico case study to substantiate the research question's premise that constitutional/administrative authority divisions shape policy effectiveness — specifically, that decentralizing spending without adequate subnational capacity/accountability safeguards (as in Seguro Popular) can produce self-undermining dynamics leading to policy collapse and recentralization, a risk directly relevant to AI/digital health adoption incentives that rely on local-level implementation capacity.

**Gap check:** Fills Bucket 1 (now 2 of 3–4 needed) and is the first source with a direct Mexico health-financing case study — closing part of the Bucket 2 LatAm-specificity gap as well, though it is about fiscal/funding federalism broadly rather than digital health/AI adoption specifically.

## Source 6 — Fabre & Straub (2023) ✅ PEER-REVIEWED, COUNTS TOWARD YOUR 10+ — FILLS BUCKET 3

- **Citation (APA 7):** Fabre, A., & Straub, S. (2023). The impact of public–private partnerships (PPPs) in infrastructure, health, and education. *Journal of Economic Literature, 61*(2), 655–715. https://doi.org/10.1257/jel.20211607
- **Source type:** Peer-reviewed journal article (comprehensive literature review/survey article)
- **Saved file:** `/Users/martinperezrodriguez/Downloads/Fabre-ImpactPublicPrivatePartnerships-2023.pdf`
- **Bucket fit:** **Bucket 3 (Incentive-based policy, PPPs, health economics/human capital)** — first confirmed peer-reviewed source for this bucket
- **Peer-review status confirmed:** Yes — published in the *Journal of Economic Literature* (American Economic Association), one of the top-ranked journals in economics; went through the AEA's standard editorial/referee process (five anonymous referees acknowledged).

### Structural Summary

**Central thesis/research question:** The paper synthesizes decades of empirical and theoretical research on public–private partnerships (PPPs) across three sectors — infrastructure (energy, transport, water/sanitation, telecom), education, and health — to assess when and why PPPs outperform, underperform, or match pure public provision, and to identify the contract design and regulatory features that drive those outcomes.

**Methodology/approach:** Structured literature review combining economic theory (incomplete-contracts framework, following Iossa and Martimort 2015) with a systematic survey of empirical evaluations (natural experiments, panel studies, cost-comparison studies) drawn from World Bank PPI and EIB databases and dozens of country-specific case studies, with heavy representation from Latin America (Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Peru, Mexico).

**Key findings:**
- PPPs now represent roughly 10% of yearly infrastructure investment by developing-country governments, but outcomes vary sharply by sector and are highly dependent on contract design, renegotiation dynamics, and regulatory capacity — not simply on public vs. private delivery.
- **Health PPPs specifically "mostly display disappointing results,"** attributed to (a) a lack of incentives for providers to improve quality of care under existing contract structures, and (b) an absence of proper diagnosis of the actual barriers to demand (i.e., contracts assume supply-side problems when demand-side barriers may dominate).
- In contrast, **education PPPs tend to increase input efficiency and deliver cost-effective learning gains**, while infrastructure PPP outcomes split along sector lines (water PPPs improved coverage/child mortality in some developing-country contexts but showed mixed pricing/efficiency effects; transport evidence is largely inconclusive or contradictory).
- Renegotiation is pervasive and often erases projected efficiency gains: in Latin America and the Caribbean, 53% of transport concessions and 76% of water concessions were renegotiated, on average within 1.6–3.1 years of signing — frequently via government-led renegotiation around elections or populist political pressure, or firm-led renegotiation via strategic underbidding.
- Standard value-for-money (VfM) evaluation methods used to justify PPPs are shown to be highly sensitive to discount-rate assumptions, meaning many "successful" PPP business cases may not hold up under more conservative assumptions.

**Relevance to the research question:** This is the strongest direct hit yet for the research question's "incentive-based public policies" framing. It provides rigorous economic evidence that **incentive structures embedded in PPP contracts are the central determinant of whether private-sector delegation improves or worsens health outcomes** — directly relevant to evaluating whether incentive-based policies designed to drive private-sector AI/digital-health adoption in emerging economies are likely to succeed. The finding that health PPPs underperform due to poorly designed provider incentives and undiagnosed demand-side barriers is a direct cautionary parallel to AI adoption policy: incentive design must address genuine adoption barriers (data governance, workforce capacity, patient trust), not just supply-side investment. The extensive Latin America case evidence (renegotiation patterns in Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Peru, Mexico) also ties directly to the research question's regional focus.

**Potential annotation angle (for independent drafting):** Use this source to establish that incentive design — not simply public vs. private delivery — determines PPP success, and that health-sector PPPs in particular suffer from misaligned provider incentives and unexamined demand-side barriers; connect this to the research question's premise that incentive-based digital-health/AI adoption policy must be carefully designed around actual adoption barriers rather than assuming private-sector involvement alone will produce better outcomes.

## Source 7 — Forrer, Kee, Newcomer, & Boyer (2010) ✅ PEER-REVIEWED, COUNTS TOWARD YOUR 10+

### Structural Summary

**Central thesis/research question:** PPPs have become a default governing model for delivering public goods and services, but their growth raises unresolved questions about *public accountability* — how to ensure PPPs still serve public-sector goals of effectiveness, efficiency, and equity when a private partner shares control. The article builds an analytical framework for assessing and strengthening PPP accountability.

**Methodology/approach:** Conceptual/framework-development article, drawing on PPP case studies, government audits, and practitioner interviews (not a quantitative empirical study). Synthesizes prior accountability literature (Goldsmith & Eggers 2004; Milward & Provan 2006) into a structured model.

**Key findings:** Proposes **six dimensions for assessing and strengthening PPP accountability**: (1) *risk* — explicitly identifying and allocating risk to the partner best able to manage it; (2) *costs and benefits* — transparently weighing fiscal and distributive trade-offs; (3) *political and social impacts*; (4) *expertise* — ensuring the public partner retains enough technical capacity to oversee the private partner rather than becoming dependent; (5) *collaboration* — building two-way communication and trust rather than treating the relationship as an arm's-length contract; (6) *performance measurement* — routinely measuring outcomes against public goals, not just outputs. The article stresses that accountability is "two-sided": both partners must take responsibility for their actions and expect that responsibility to be recognized, and the public partner must retain a "leadership role" / "upper hand" to ensure public interests remain central even as both parties develop interdependence.

**Relevance to the research question:** Directly supplies an accountability-and-governance framework that complements Fabre & Straub's (2023) economic efficiency findings — where Fabre & Straub show incentive misalignment causes health PPPs to underperform, Forrer et al. supply the governance mechanism (the six dimensions, especially *expertise* and *performance measurement*) through which incentive-based policy could be designed to prevent that misalignment. This is directly transferable to evaluating incentive-based AI/digital-health adoption policy: a government incentivizing private-sector AI adoption needs retained technical expertise and routine performance measurement to avoid losing oversight capacity to its private partners — a direct expression of the capstone's "M&E-by-design" argument.

## Source 9 — Kula & Fryatt (2014) ✅ PEER-REVIEWED, COUNTS TOWARD YOUR 10+
