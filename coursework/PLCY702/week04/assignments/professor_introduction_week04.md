## Thematic Commentary

### On the Peace Pact: The Articles of Confederation

The Articles of Confederation—our first Constitution—sets the stage for the impracticability of the government after the Declaration. It would be the invocation of the Declaration that would lead to the Constitution we have today—the second such use of the Declaration's ideas. 

The actual history of the Union is a thorn in the side of revisionists, social justice warriors, and anyone else who claims the vague argument of systemic issues with the Founding. The actual history and the words of the Founders themselves cast much shade on their unsupported assertions. This book is but one of many that does so. 

Federalism looms large—partly national; partly federal. You should pick up *Miracle in Philadelphia* for another excellent account of all this. The bottom line (noted in Federalist #13) is that **if the union would not have been formed, we would have been conquered by sections from a foreign power who, had no statement on things like slavery (its abhorrence).** 

Near the end of the book, there is a cautionary tale from Adams. He and Jefferson were among the grandest thinkers of our time. More from Adams can be read here at the American Mind. **Did he predict our current predicament?** The question in our post constitutional era—a grave time indeed—is whether a people can ever become one people, or whether we are inevitably all tribal.

### On De Tocqueville: Chapter 5

Chapter 5 arrives! What is the sovereignty of a people? How is it expressed? De T speaks of the people from the small to the large. States (localities) were still quite strong. Are they today? Maybe, but as De T notes, this is a fragile thing. 

Last week, a small town in Michigan removed the entire Republican town council because they accepted the development of a Chinese EV factory that included in its articles of incorporation a commitment to the CCP. The people rose up. They ran a slate of unaffiliated replacements. They swept in, the prior removed, and then changed the locks of the government buildings. **This is an example of what De T speaks of still alive, but the example in Michigan notes how rare this in fact is in our modern times.**

De T has some fascinating things to write about administration—is it true today? Do we not care so much about it as in 1830? Yet of all the great insights he has, note his combining of governmental centralization with administrative centralization. **How do they combine and mutually assist?!**

### On McClellan: Critical Reflections

Herein we find problems out of the gate. There was no alleged problem with the first part of the Declaration—it was an expression of the American Mind, that Jefferson said himself he took from Sydney, Cicero, and Aristotle. Does this cause confusion? Not quite, but McClellan thinks so. 

More problematic is McClellan's claim that Davis found support for the Confederacy, when in fact, in his Cornerstone speech, he claimed the exact opposite of McClellan. Such is the problems with not being a widely read man. If you read the Lincoln debates in 1858, you will see a documentary assault on his opponent backed up by actual words of the Founders to support his position. 

McClellan actually rides right off the rails when he compares Aristotle to Natural law theory of Aquinas, despite the fact that Aquinas was hundreds of years after Aristotle. How do we know this? Behold Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics*:

> "Now some think that all justice is of this sort, because that which is by nature is unchangeable and has everywhere the same force (as fire burns both here and in Persia), while they see change in the things recognized as just. This, however, is not true in this unqualified way, but is true in a sense; or rather, with the gods it is perhaps not true at all, while with us there is something that is just even by nature, yet all of it is changeable; but still some is by nature, some not by nature. It is evident which sort of thing, among things capable of being otherwise, is by nature, and which is not but is legal and conventional, assuming that both are equally changeable. And in all other things the same distinction will apply; by nature the right hand is stronger, yet it is possible that all men should come to be ambidextrous. The things which are just by virtue of convention and expediency are like measures; for wine and corn measures are not everywhere equal, but larger in wholesale and smaller in retail markets. Similarly, the things which are just not by nature but by human enactment are not everywhere the same, since constitutions also are not the same, though there is but one which is everywhere by nature the best. Of things just and lawful each is related as the universal to its particulars; for the things that are done are many, but of them each is one, since it is universal."

This is Natural Right not Natural Law. While it is true that Cicero dabbles in this topic a bit, it is unclear that he goes as far as Aquinas wants to take him. Needless to say that all things are changeable, does not mean ALL things—it means that justice depends on circumstances in light of Nature—that is, what you do in one situation or another is guided by nature, but all action is not guided by a law subscribing to only one action. 

In this sense, McClellan floats an airball again when speaking of Locke—because Locke was employing natural right in his treatise for the given time he lived, for a more just regime. While natural rights have some similarity to natural law, much less or more, the compatibility is present. 

**All of the above is to make this point: never simply trust an academic. Read the primary documents.**

Those, like Garrison, who hated the Constitution were in actual agreement with slaveholders on the matter. Knowing this, puts the arguments in complete context. That McClellan buys into this incorrect argument is the same as those on the left (or in that time), the slave oligarchy. This forces McClellan to run the intellectual argument to its conclusion (at least he's honest): Some women could vote, so could some free blacks. Even more astonishing, there were black slaveowners. Conveniently, this is all left out of McClellan. 

Another sleight of hand: the cartoon on pp. 137–138 is from 1850. This is an anachronistic representation of the Founders where, they believed no such thing. But by 1850, after the 1830s debates on slavery and under the influence of Hegel (where elite slaveholder children went to school in Europe and became enamored with historicism), hardened their hearts to the institution.

**There is much more to say here, but maybe your interest is piqued for further study.**

On the Philadelphia Convention, I leave you to your devices, armed with the appeal to read critically. *Miracle in Philadelphia*, plus the actual records of the debates, should be your more appropriate guides. But know McClellan's arguments.

## Meditation: On Byzantine Christian Practice and the Nature of Active Participation

I have been thinking about two somewhat different but related things. The first is the practice of the early Byzantine (that is Eastern) Christians—the original Christians. We have long lost what THEY did in the west. The first instance is something that someone pointed out to me, that I vaguely knew about, but never really thought of too much. The Muslims borrowed this practice (not meant as a slight) from the Christian East—that is the simple submission (the bowing or prostration) in prayer. It is meant to align the mind and body to God. 

Russia has kept one remnant of an accoutrement of this practice: the *podruchnik*. The believer would put their hands on this when prostrating (usually several times during services). It was meant to keep the hands clean as a practical measure. 

Which leads me to the second point—None of the original churches had pews. NONE. The invention of them comes from the Americanization of the religious (is it a progressive innovation?). There are several reasons why most Eastern Orthodox have pews today, but it never really started until the 20th century. Traditionally, you will see Orthodox Churches look like this: a wide open space—the people would STAND. Chairs may be around the outer part of the church for people who need them, but the sanctuary was always open. 

The reason was, it made the believer more active in the liturgy, whereas sitting, you are isolated and passive. There was more room for the bowing and praying the believers engaged in in this setting rather than the setting with pews. The practice of aligning oneself—using all the senses—is a way to transform everything in you, to die to self. This process takes a LONG time and is ever ongoing.

**Why does this matter?** Consider the parallel to civic participation: in early America, citizens stood at town meetings, voted by voice and assemblage, participated physically and vocally in their own governance. Modern "participation" often means checking a box, sitting passively, being an observer rather than an actor. The question for this week—especially as you read De Tocqueville's reflections on the township and local sovereignty—is whether we have lost not just the *forms* of participation, but the *transformative nature* of active engagement with one's community and government.

## On Pentecost: The Church and the Coming of the Holy Spirit

The Church this week celebrates Pentecost. The tradition of the Church—that is what was passed down in speech and in writing—by the Apostles, and by those they chose, is explained as follows:

After the Saviour's Ascension into the Heavens, the eleven Apostles and the rest of His disciples, the God-loving women who followed after Him from the beginning, His Mother, the most holy Virgin Mary, and His brethren—all together about 120 souls—returned from the Mount of Olives to Jerusalem. Entering into the house where they gathered, they went into the upper room, and there they persevered in prayer and supplication, awaiting the coming of the Holy Spirit, as their Divine Teacher had promised them. In the meanwhile, they chose Matthias, who was elected to take the place of Judas among the Apostles.

Thus, on this day, the seventh Sunday of Pascha, the tenth day after the Ascension and the fiftieth day after Pascha, at the third hour of the day from the rising of the sun, there suddenly came a sound from Heaven, as when a mighty wind blows, and it filled the whole house where the Apostles and the rest with them were gathered. Immediately after the sound, there appeared tongues of fire that divided and rested upon the head of each one. Filled with the Spirit, all those present began speaking not in their native tongue, but in other tongues and dialects, as the Holy Spirit instructed them.

The multitudes that had come together from various places for the feast, most of whom were Jews by race and religion, were called Parthians, Medes, Elamites, and so forth, according to the places where they dwelt. Though they spoke many different tongues, they were present in Jerusalem by divine dispensation. When they heard that sound that came down from Heaven to the place where the disciples of Christ were gathered, all ran together to learn what had taken place. But they were confounded when they came and heard the Apostles speaking in their own tongues. Marveling at this, they said one to another, "Behold, are not all these which speak Galileans? And how hear we every man in his own tongue, wherein we were born?" But others, because of their foolishness and excess of evil, mocked the wonder and said that the Apostles were drunken.

Then Peter stood up with the eleven, and raising his voice, spoke to all the people, proving that that which had taken place was not drunkenness, but the fulfilment of God's promise that had been spoken by the Prophet Joel: "And it shall come to pass in the last days, that I shall pour out of My Spirit upon all flesh, and your sons and daughters shall prophesy" (Joel 2:28), and he preached Jesus of Nazareth unto them, proving in many ways that He is Christ the Lord, Whom the Jews crucified but God raised from the dead. On hearing Peter's teaching, many were smitten with compunction and received the word. Thus, they were baptized, and on that day about three thousand souls were added to the Faith of Christ.

Such, therefore, are the reasons for today's feast: the coming of the All-holy Spirit into the world, the completion of the Lord Jesus Christ's promise, and the fulfilment of the hope of the sacred disciples, which we celebrate today. This is the final feast of the great mystery and dispensation of God's incarnation. On this last, and great, and saving day of Pentecost, the Apostles of the Saviour, who were unlearned fishermen, made wise now of a sudden by the Holy Spirit, clearly and with divine authority spoke the heavenly doctrines. They became heralds of the truth and teachers of the whole world. On this day they were ordained and began their apostleship, of which the salvation of those three thousand souls in one day was the comely and marvellous first fruit.

**Not many people know that those who came after the Apostles, left their own accounts and comments. This was all collected in the *Patrologia Graeca*. You have to know Greek to read it! But they were translated some time ago in the Fathers of the Church series—including the Nicene, and post Nicene writings of those who came after the Apostles.**
