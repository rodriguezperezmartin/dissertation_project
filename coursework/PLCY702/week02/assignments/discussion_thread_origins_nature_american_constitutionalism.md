## Assignment Prompt (Official)

> How were the people of the American colonies constituted? What social, religious, and/or political institutions or ideas bound them together? How did their written founding documents rely on or alter these pre-existing bonds?

## Discussion Thread

The foundational beliefs of the American people long predated the drafting and ratification of our United States Constitution in 1787. These ideologies were forged by early pilgrims, gold seekers, farmers, and religious nonconformists who sought freedoms they believed were granted by God rather than by European barons, lords, or the well-established aristocracy (McClellan, 2000). The early American colonies were not populated by a singular British culture, but rather by various subcultures that sought specific political and religious identities. This landscape was shaped by the distinct ways of the Puritans, Cavaliers, Quakers, and Scots-Irish. Each of these groups carried their own unique social constitutions into the New World, laying the foundation for a decentralized, consent-based republic.

### How the People of the American Colonies Were Constituted

The people of the American colonies were constituted organically through their various distinct cultural and religious migrations rather than by a single top-down political decree from the British Crown. Each of the colonies was populated by distinct subcultures with entirely different views on liberty and authority (Fischer, 1989). For example, the Puritans of New England viewed themselves as God's chosen people, seeking to establish a new Zion through a purified church-state that emphasized ordered, communal liberty. In contrast, the Quakers of Pennsylvania deeply resented old social hierarchies and constituted themselves around radical spiritual equality and reciprocal liberty. Further inland, the Scots-Irish, who populated much of the Appalachian region, fiercely resented elite ruling classes, thus bringing a combative, natural liberty that would later shape the frontier and the American Revolution (Fischer, 1989; Ross, n.d.).

Despite these various cultural differences, these groups utilized a similar conceptual framework to constitute themselves politically. As Donald S. Lutz (1988) explains, early colonists organized their foundings around a specific covenantal formula: (1) explaining why the document was needed, (2) actively creating a "people," (3) defining their shared values and interests, (4) establishing a government, and (5) specifying the exact formation of that government. Furthermore, as Alexis de Tocqueville observed, this point of departure in local, self-governing townships embedded absolute democratic and republican theories into the DNA of the colonists long before a national government was conceived (Tocqueville, 1835/2010). Thus, the American people were constituted through deliberate and localized acts of mutual consent.

### Social, Religious, and Political Institutions that Bound Them Together

Religion was the primary social glue of the early colonies. Early immigrants viewed themselves as a new version of the Old Testament Jews, continually returning to the Word of God to find His will and establish their roots in a new, uncultivated land—the Americas (Lutz, 1988). A deep devotion to God and a zeal for righteousness defined their communal obligations. As John Winthrop expressed in his letters regarding the Massachusetts Bay Company, "What can be a better worke, & more honorable & worthy a Christian than to help raise & supporte a particular Church while it is in the Infancy, & to ioyne his forces with such a company of faithful people" (Winthrop, 1630/1869, p. 310).

Politically, these religious bonds were translated into civil institutions through written documents like the Mayflower Compact, the Fundamental Orders of Connecticut, and the Massachusetts Body of Liberties. The Mayflower Compact functioned as a literal social contract, transforming a religious congregation into a civil body politic. Over time, these theological compacts evolved into enduring political institutions. They bound the colonists together through the establishment of local elected assemblies, independent courts, and a fierce reliance on the English common law to protect property and civic rights against arbitrary royal governors (McClellan, 2000). The shared experience of participating in these local representative assemblies created a unified civic identity that transcended their distinct theological origins.

### How Founding Documents Relied On or Altered Pre-Existing Bonds

The written founding documents of 1776 and 1787 did not invent American political rights from thin air; rather, they relied heavily on these pre-existing localized bonds. The U.S. Constitution scaled the logic and preliminary framework of the local Puritan township covenant up to a continental framework. It did so while maintaining the fundamental premise that all legitimate governments require the consent and approval of the governed (Lutz, 1988). Furthermore, the Constitution relied on the colonists' centuries-old habit of operating under written charters, adopting the familiar British model of bicameral legislatures and a separation of powers (McClellan, 2000).

Yet, the founding documents transformed these traditional ties in two critical ways. First, they shifted the covenant from a religious obligation to a secular one. While early colonial agreements depended on divine enforcement, the U.S. Constitution replaced this with a secular political compact enforced by a supreme judiciary and structural checks and balances. Second, the Constitution had to alter the fierce, independent subcultures whose values, religions, and ways of life were highly distinct. By implementing federalism, the founding documents bound these subcultures—which consisted of the Puritans, the Quakers, the Cavaliers, and the Scots-Irish—into a single sovereign union. To ensure the survival of this new nation composed of these distinct subgroups, the principle of federalism required that political power be divided between a central national government and individual state governments. This ensured that these sub-groups retained their distinct regional identities, local institutions, and religious customs while operating under one supreme national law.
