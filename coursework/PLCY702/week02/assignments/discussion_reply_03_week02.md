## Reply

Daniel, your post was very insightful. It induced thoughts regarding the success of early American constitutionalism. I feel this success indeed lied in the principle of prefabricated community bonds. As Donald S. Lutz (1988) argues, a political constitution cannot succeed unless a "people" has already been formed through shared moral and cultural commitments. The various colonies were already assembled into distinct communities that lived by deep-rooted beliefs, each utilizing their own charters and systematic processes for daily life. For example, the Puritans believed in the necessity of a civil magistrate to conduct marriage ceremonies to ensure the union was legally binding and orderly within the community (Fischer, 1989, p. 81).

When we compare these distinct colonies and their roots to other historical regimes, it is easy to see why certain types of governments have failed. Rome, operating under a spirit of dominion and conquest, did not bequeath conquered nations with the choice or consent to accept its ruling. Similarly, China's Warring States period during the Zhou dynasty consisted of a fractured nation governed by lords constantly pillaging and battling one another. These methods of government were not founded upon the same localized, consent-based principles as our American colonies—principles of love, mutual respect, order, obedience, and a shared sense of accountability to a higher power—and thus, they ultimately failed.

If those warring states, with their different dialects and customs, had been allowed to establish their own local governments with a degree of regional sovereignty—much like the distinct American colonies—the outcome may have been more favorable, and that dynasty might have found enduring success. However, such decentralized, federalist systems of government were still in the very distant future.

Ultimately, the foundational notion taught by Christ—"Therefore all things whatsoever ye would that men should do to you, do ye even so to them" (Matthew 7:12, King James Version)—embodies the very philosophies that were needed, and which our forefathers brought with them to the founding of America.
