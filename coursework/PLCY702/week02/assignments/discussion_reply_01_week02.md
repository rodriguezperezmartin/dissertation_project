## Reply

Robert, your synthesis of Fischer and Lutz brilliantly highlights how the American colonies were constituted organically before any formal national government was established. I would argue that the very existence of Fischer's (1989) four distinct British folkways is precisely what made our constitutional principles—those of federalism, separation of powers, and state sovereignty—an absolute necessity.

If the American colonists had simply been a monolithic, homogeneous group of English citizens, a highly centralized, unitary government might have sufficed and been sought after. However, the profound cultural and theological paradox—their "difference of differences"—is exactly what unified them in the objective of framing a decentralized constitution. They required a federalist framework that allowed them to unite for mutual defense and commerce without sacrificing their fiercely guarded regional identities and local autonomy.

From this, we can see the unique strength of this decentralized, liberty-focused origin when comparing it to other constitutional foundings, such as the Mexican Constitution of 1917. As the Library of Congress (n.d.) notes, the 1917 Mexican framework was largely constructed by a highly educated middle class focused heavily on engineering sweeping social, agrarian, and economic rights. While an important historical document, its foundational premise differed vastly from our American emphasis on unalienable rights, specifically "Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness" (U.S. Declaration of Independence, 1776). Because the Mexican Constitution did not spawn from the same decentralized "forges of fire" or the cultural necessity to protect diverse, pre-existing regional liberties, it has historically struggled to deliver the same enduring constitutional stability and strict separation of powers found in the U.S. framework.

The American framework has succeeded because it accommodated existing cultural realities rather than trying to engineer a homogeneous society from the top down. As we ponder and consider how federalism has protected the distinct cultures of early America, what are your thoughts regarding the modern attempt to unify a highly diverse population under a central framework—the European Union—and its lack of a shared foundational "covenant" required for the long-term stability which we have experienced in our country?
