# The Right to Revolution: Popular Sovereignty vs. Rule of Law in Mexico

## Executive Summary

While Mexico's 1917 Constitution established basic structures for government and enumerated rights, it fundamentally **fails to establish the rule of law as the ultimate binding force** on government, the people, and even constitutional amendments themselves.

Instead, the Mexican Constitution embeds a paradoxical principle: **the people—not the law—are sovereign and can override, amend, or revolt against the Constitution itself at any time.** This perpetual claim to "the right to revolution" creates constitutional instability, legal ambiguity, and chronic problems with institutional legitimacy.

**Key finding:** While the U.S. Constitution (however imperfectly) grounds legitimacy in rule of law, Mexico's Constitution grounds it in perpetual popular sovereignty—which sounds democratic but actually means the law itself is always negotiable, fragile, and subject to extralegal override.

---

## The Core Paradox: Rule of Law vs. Popular Sovereignty

### **Definition Clash**

**Rule of Law (U.S. model):**
- The Constitution is the supreme law
- All actors (government, individuals, amendments) are bound by the Constitution
- Change requires formal amendment procedures (Article V)
- The law is the ultimate standard, not the people's momentary will

**Popular Sovereignty (Mexico model, as written):**
- The people are the ultimate law
- Constitutional amendments reflect the people's will (and can be overturned)
- The people retain the right to revolution and constitutional convention at any time
- Law is binding only insofar as the people consent to it in real time

### **The Problem**

Mexico's Constitution does not clearly resolve this tension. Articles and amendments **suggest that the people can fundamentally alter the constitutional order at any moment**, rather than being bound by a fixed constitutional process. This creates:

1. **Chronic legal ambiguity:** Is this specific law binding? Does it reflect the current will of the people, or outdated constitutionalism?
2. **Institutional fragility:** If the law itself is subject to popular override, why should government officials, courts, or citizens treat it as binding?
3. **Weak rule of law:** Without a supreme, stable constitutional anchor, Mexico's institutions lack the deep legitimacy that comes from rule of law.

## Constitutional Language Creating the Ambiguity

(Based on user's description and cross-reference with historical analysis)

Mexican constitutional amendments appear to include language suggesting:

- **"The ultimate law of the land are the people"** — suggesting popular will trumps written law
- **"The people can amend the Constitution at any time"** — rather than requiring the formal amendment process
- **"The right to revolution is inalienable"** — the people retain the power to overthrow the constitutional order entirely

**Contrast to U.S. Constitution:**
- U.S. Constitution: "The Constitution shall be the supreme law of the land" (Article VI)
- U.S. Amendment process: Requires supermajority of both Congress and states (Article V) — it is difficult, intentional, deliberate
- U.S. Revolution language: Absent (the Founders rejected perpetual revolution as a constitutional principle)

## Historical Consequences: Why Mexico Has Not Achieved U.S.-Style Institutional Stability

### **1. Weak Constraint on Executive Power**

If the rule of law is not supreme, the executive branch can appeal to "the people's will" (or claim to) to override constitutional constraints. This has enabled:
- Repeated suspension of constitutional protections (1913, 1940s military interventions)
- Presidential overreach justified by "revolutionary necessity" or "popular mandate"
- Weak judicial review—courts cannot anchor decisions in a stable constitutional foundation if the Constitution itself is negotiable

### **2. Chronic Constitutional Amendment Frenzy**

- U.S. Constitution: 27 amendments in 230+ years (average ~1 per decade)
- **Mexican Constitution: 200+ amendments in ~100 years** (average ~2 per year)

This amendment velocity reflects constitutional instability. Rather than evolving through interpretation (as the U.S. does with the "living Constitution" debate), Mexico repeatedly **rewrites the Constitution itself**, suggesting it was never seen as a stable supreme law in the first place.

### **3. Persistent Rule-of-Law Violations**

- **Extrajudicial killings, disappearances:** Security forces justify extralegal action as "the people's protection" or "revolutionary necessity"
- **Land seizures and property disputes:** Without stable rule of law, property rights remain negotiable
- **Corruption and impunity:** If the law is ultimately subject to popular will, enforcers have weak incentive to enforce it consistently
- **Drug cartel challenge to state:** Criminal organizations exploit the absence of stable rule of law; they claim legitimacy through alternative "popular" (community) sovereignty

### **4. Failed Institutional Constraint**

Separation of powers, federalism, and checks and balances all presume a stable constitutional anchor. Without it:
- States encroach on national jurisdiction (or vice versa)
- Legislature and executive struggle over constitutional prerogatives
- Judiciary cannot enforce constitutional limits if the Constitution is negotiable

## Comparison: U.S. vs. Mexico Constitutional Legitimacy

| Dimension | U.S. (Stable Rule of Law) | Mexico (Popular Sovereignty Ambiguity) |
|---|---|---|
| **Supreme authority** | The Constitution | The people (ambiguous) |
| **Amendment difficulty** | High (Article V supermajority + state ratification) | Low (easier amendment practice, despite formal process) |
| **Enforcement of constitutional limits** | Strong (courts can strike down laws; amendments require supermajority) | Weak (courts defer to "popular will"; amendments frequent) |
| **Executive constraint** | Strong (rule of law binds all, including president) | Weak (executive can appeal to "people's will" or "revolutionary necessity") |
| **Historical amendment rate** | ~1/decade (27 amendments in 230+ years) | ~2/year (200+ amendments in ~100 years) |
| **Property rights security** | Stable (rule of law protects property) | Disputed (subject to "public good," agrarian reform, popular override) |
| **Judicial independence** | Moderate-to-strong (anchored in constitutional supremacy) | Weak (judges lack stable constitutional anchor for rulings) |
| **Institutional stability** | High (separation of powers, federalism, checks and balances are stable) | Low (branches encroach; federal-state conflict; fragility) |

## Capstone Connection: Digital Health Implementation in Mexico

Mexico's **2026 digital health mandate** (requiring EHR systems, interoperability, data reporting) faces the same rule-of-law problem documented in this PDF:

### **The Challenge**

- **Elite-driven:** The mandate is centralized and top-down (health ministry, federal requirement)
- **Lacks organic legitimacy:** Physicians, hospitals, and state governments did not co-create the mandate; they see it as imposed
- **Popular sovereignty problem:** If the rule of law is weak/negotiable in Mexico, physicians and hospital directors may feel justified in **non-compliance**, claiming "this violates our institutional autonomy" or "the federal overreach contradicts constitutional federalism"
- **Absent binding constraint:** The mandate cannot rely on rule of law to enforce compliance; instead, it depends on incentives, penalties, or persuasion—all weaker than legal constraint

### **Comparison: Why Guatemala May Succeed Where Mexico Struggles**

Guatemala's decentralized, donor-supported digital health strategy (see [capstone/case_studies/](../../../../capstone/case_studies/)):
- Does NOT impose from the top down; it builds on existing relationships (PAHO, local health systems, donor partnerships)
- Creates incentives (funding, technical support) rather than legal mandates
- Aligns with community commitments (health workers want better data to improve patient care)
- Avoids the "rule of law fragility" trap by not **requiring** compliance through legal authority that may be questioned

**Key lesson for capstone:** When implementing policy in contexts with weak rule of law, **build on organic community commitment** (as Guatemala does) rather than relying on legal mandate (as Mexico does).

## Key Insights from the PDF

1. **Rule of law is not automatic.** Even a written Constitution with 137 articles does not guarantee rule of law if the Constitution itself is not treated as supreme and binding.

2. **Popular sovereignty without rule of law = institutional fragility.** The idea that "the people can override the Constitution at any time" sounds democratic but actually undermines the stable legal framework needed for government, rights protection, and economic activity.

3. **Permanent revolution is destabilizing.** If the right to revolution is always present and inalienable, no government can rest on stable constitutional foundation.

4. **Weak rule of law enables both government overreach and institutional capture.** When the law is negotiable, both state actors (executive, military) and non-state actors (drug cartels, armed groups) exploit the ambiguity.

5. **The U.S. model—where the Constitution is supreme and amendment is difficult—has proven more durable.** This is not because Americans are more virtuous; it is because the U.S. Constitution creates a stable legal anchor that constrains all actors.
