# James McClellan - Liberty, Order, and Justice (Part 2)

Document 1: McClellan, Liberty, Order, and Justice (Part 2, pp. 89–107)

Executive Summary This section traces the institutional evolution of America’s first constitutions, arguing that the American colonists were politically constituted long before the 1787 Philadelphia Convention. By analyzing early colonial governance—from corporate and proprietary charters to formal royal colonies—McClellan demonstrates that the colonies operated under a shared, organically developed governing pattern rooted deeply in English common law. These early frameworks established the administrative and legal scaffolding (such as bicameralism and localized voting requirements) that directly informed the state constitutions drafted after 1776.

Student Notes & Key Takeaways

Uniform Institutional Blueprint: Despite their distinct cultural and religious origins, all thirteen colonies eventually exhibited a remarkably similar governing structure featuring a chief executive (governor) and a bicameral legislature. This mirrored the British mixed constitution, balancing monarchical, aristocratic, and democratic elements, which conditioned the colonists to expect a separation of institutional powers (McClellan, p. 98).

Executive vs. Legislative Friction: Colonial governors were largely appointed by the Crown or proprietors, while the lower houses of the assemblies were elected by the colonists. This structural divide created an early and enduring colonial suspicion of centralized executive power. The colonists came to view their local elected assemblies as the true, legitimate voice of the people, fiercely defending their right to control taxation and internal laws (McClellan, pp. 89–107).

The Charter Tradition: The colonists did not invent constitutionalism from abstract Enlightenment philosophy; they were already accustomed to written charters outlining their rights and boundaries. The Mayflower Compact (1620) served as the seminal political covenant establishing civil body politic by consent, while the Fundamental Orders of Connecticut (1639) functioned as the first true written constitution in America, explicitly outlining a framework of government without requiring the authorization of a monarch (McClellan, pp. 89–107).

Restrictive Early Suffrage: Early colonial civic participation was heavily restricted. The right to vote was not viewed as a universal human right but as a civic privilege contingent upon a "stake in society." This meant specific religious affiliations (initially) and substantial property ownership were required, under the belief that only those with independent wealth could vote without being coerced by employers or landlords (McClellan, pp. 89–107).

The Long Arc of Republicanism: The modern, universal right to vote evolved over two centuries. Initially fiercely guarded by local and state authorities to maintain cultural cohesion, voting rights were progressively federalized through subsequent constitutional amendments (e.g., the 15th, 19th, 24th, and 26th). This shifted electoral sovereignty from distinct local communities increasingly to the national level (McClellan, pp. 89–107).
