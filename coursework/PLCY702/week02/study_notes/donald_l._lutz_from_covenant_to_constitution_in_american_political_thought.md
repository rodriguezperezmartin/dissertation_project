# Donald L. Lutz - From Covenant to Constitution in American Political Thought

Document 4: Donald L. Lutz, From Covenant to Constitution in American Political Thought

Executive Summary Lutz traces the conceptual and historical evolution of modern American constitutionalism directly back to early Protestant covenant theology. He demonstrates how colonists systematically adapted the theological concept of a covenant—a binding, sacred agreement made before God—into a secular political compact. This organic transformation from religious covenants to civil compacts, and ultimately to formal state constitutions, proves that American constitutionalism was a gradual, indigenous development of local self-government, rather than an abrupt invention of the Enlightenment or the 1787 Convention.

Student Notes & Key Takeaways

The Theological Roots of Consent: Early documents like the Mayflower Compact were strict covenants. A community bound itself together morally and politically under divine witness, establishing the radical premise that legitimate civil authority requires the mutual, recorded consent of the governed, rather than divine right granted to a king (Lutz).

Evolution from Covenant to Compact: Over several decades, these religious covenants secularized into political compacts. As colonial populations diversified, documents maintained the structural logic of mutual consent and binding law, but God was gradually removed as the active enforcer. The focus shifted strictly toward civil administration, property, and secular rights (Lutz).

Organic Scaling of Constitutionalism: The U.S. Constitution is the direct institutional descendant of these early colonial documents. The Framers did not invent a new system in 1787; they effectively scaled the foundational logic of the local, consent-based township covenant up to a massive continental federal framework, replacing God as the enforcer with a supreme, written judicial text (Lutz).
