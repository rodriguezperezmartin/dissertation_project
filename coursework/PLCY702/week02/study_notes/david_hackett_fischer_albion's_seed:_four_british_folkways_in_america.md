# David Hackett Fischer - Albion's Seed: Four British Folkways in America

Document 3: David Hackett Fischer, Albion's Seed: Four British Folkways in America

Executive Summary Fischer’s seminal work dismantles the myth of a monolithic "British" settlement of North America. He argues that American political culture was profoundly shaped by four distinct waves of English migration between 1629 and 1775. Each subculture originated from different regions of Britain and carried distinct "folkways," encompassing religious beliefs, family structures, and fundamentally different definitions of liberty and authority. These pre-existing cultural foundations established the intense regional identities that the U.S. Constitution later had to carefully balance through federalism.

Student Notes & Key Takeaways

The Puritans (Massachusetts Bay, pp. 25–38): Migrating from East Anglia, this highly educated, middle-class group valued ordered liberty. Their society was highly structured, centering on congregational self-rule, strict moral conformity, and town meetings. Liberty meant the freedom to do what is right and good within a tight-knit community, not individual autonomy.

The Cavaliers (Virginia, pp. 157–181): Aristocratic royalists from southern England fleeing the English Civil War. They recreated a highly hierarchical agrarian society modeled on English country life. They relied on primogeniture, indentured servitude, and eventually race-based slavery. They valued hegemonic liberty—the freedom to rule others and be the absolute master of one's own domain without outside interference.

The Quakers (Delaware Valley/PA, pp. 308–327): Migrating from the North Midlands, this sect emphasized radical egalitarianism and spiritual equality. Because they believed in the "Inner Light" of God within every person, they valued reciprocal liberty—a steadfast belief in freedom of conscience for all, leading to early abolitionist sentiments and peaceful relations with Native Americans.

The Scots-Irish (Appalachian Backcountry, pp. 442–460): Borderland peoples fleeing centuries of warfare and poverty in Northern Britain. They organized by extended family clans rather than strict civic structures. Highly suspicious of centralized, elite authority, they valued natural liberty—a fierce, combative independence that deeply influenced the culture of the American frontier and the Continental Army.
