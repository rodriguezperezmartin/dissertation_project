# Dr. Jason Ross - Week 2 Video

Document 7: Dr. Jason Ross, Week 2 Video Script

Executive Summary Dr. Ross synthesizes the week's readings to address how the American colonists constituted themselves before drafting formal written documents. Leveraging Fischer’s Albion’s Seed, Ross explains that America was formed by four distinct English subcultures, meaning the "people" existed before the "parchment." The lecture highlights that the legitimacy of the American constitutional order stems directly from its ability to reflect, rather than invent, this pre-existing social unity. This raises the enduring public policy question of whether a constitution can succeed if it attempts to radically transform a society rather than reflect its established values.

Student Notes & Key Takeaways

People Before Parchment: Colonists constituted themselves as distinct, self-aware political bodies with shared cultures, religious views, and political habits long before the Declaration of Independence or the U.S. Constitution were drafted. The legal documents merely formalized what already existed culturally (Ross, Week 2 Script).

Quaker Radicalism vs. Puritan Strictness: Ross points out the vast ideological differences within the colonies. William Penn's Charter of Liberties in Pennsylvania was a radical experiment in democracy and freedom of conscience (protecting anyone who believed in God), contrasting sharply with the Puritans' desire for a singular, purified, exclusionary church-state (Ross, Week 2 Script).

Cavalier Hierarchy in Virginia: Ross contrasts the democratic tendencies of New England and Pennsylvania with Virginia's Laws Divine, Moral and Martial (1611). Virginia recreated the aristocratic structures of England, enforcing brutal capital punishments for blasphemy and establishing deeply hierarchical systems to sustain a ruling elite in an environment not naturally suited for aristocracy (Ross, Week 2 Script).

The Legitimacy Question: A key reason historical constitutional documents were held legitimate is that they reflected a pre-existing social unity. Ross challenges students to consider a core policy dilemma: To what degree can a constitution or policy framework be effective if it attempts to completely reorganize or transform a political community from the top down, rather than reflecting its established, organic values? (Ross, Week 2 Script).
