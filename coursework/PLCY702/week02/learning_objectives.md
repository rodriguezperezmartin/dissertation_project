# Week 2 — Learning Objectives & Study Guide

## Overview (Module 2)
**Topic:** The Origins and Nature of American Constitutionalism

**Guiding Questions:** How were the people of the American colonies constituted? What social, religious, and/or political institutions or ideas bound them together? How did their written founding documents rely on or alter these pre-existing bonds?

This week shifts from *defining* constitutionalism (Week 1) to examining the **specific institutions and ideas that bound the American colonies together** *prior to* the 1787 Constitutional Convention, and how the Founding-era documents (Declaration, state constitutions, the U.S. Constitution) either preserved or transformed those pre-existing bonds.

**Key Insight:** The colonists and founding generation constituted themselves as distinct peoples long *before* their written documents. As Dr. Ross notes: "People Before Parchment" — the parchment (constitutional documents) formalized what already existed culturally. This raises a crucial policy question: **Can a constitution succeed if it attempts to radically transform a society from the top down, rather than reflect its established, organic values?**

**Required Materials:** 13 readings + 1 video lecture

## Learning Outcomes
Upon successful completion of this week's study and discussion participation, you will be able to:

1. **Identify pre-existing constitutional foundations** — Explain how the English colonies developed distinct political and social identities (Puritan, Quaker, Cavalier, Scots-Irish) *before* formal constitutional documents.
2. **Contrast colonial constitutional approaches** — Compare and contrast the political philosophies embedded in founding documents (Mayflower Compact, state constitutions, Penn's Charter of Liberties) and how they reflected different social/religious foundations.
3. **Connect theology to political theory** — Analyze how Protestant covenant theology, Puritan millennialism, Quaker egalitarianism, and other religious traditions shaped constitutional thought.
4. **Evaluate constitutional legitimacy** — Assess the relationship between a constitution's legitimacy and its ability to reflect (vs. transform) pre-existing community bonds.
5. **Apply to policy** — Identify how this historical analysis informs contemporary debates about constitutional interpretation, institutional reform, and policy implementation.

## Discussion Thread Framework — "People Before Parchment"

**Assignment Prompt (Official):**
> How were the people of the American colonies constituted? What social, religious, and/or political institutions or ideas bound them together? How did their written founding documents rely on or alter these pre-existing bonds?

**Your Thread Must Address All Three Sub-Questions:**

1. **"How were the colonies constituted?"**
   - *What does this mean?* The colonists organized themselves as distinct political/social communities long before formal written constitutions.
   - *Use Albion's Seed:* The four English subcultures (Puritans, Quakers, Cavaliers, Scots-Irish) brought distinct values, governance models, and social hierarchies to North America.
   - *Key sources:* Fischer, Lutz, Dr. Ross video.

2. **"What institutions/ideas bound them together?"**
   - *Institutions:* Church, family, local government, militia, colonial assemblies.
   - *Ideas:* Covenant theology (Puritan), the Inner Light (Quaker), aristocratic hierarchy (Cavalier), clan/family loyalty (Scots-Irish), English common law (all).
   - *Key sources:* Winthrop, Penn's Charter of Liberties, Fundamental Orders of Connecticut, Frohnen, Lutz.

3. **"How did founding documents rely on or alter these bonds?"**
   - *Relied on:* Documents formalized existing community identity (Mayflower Compact, state constitutions).
   - *Altered:* Some documents attempted to transform society (e.g., Virginia's aristocratic model vs. Quaker democracy).
   - *The legitimacy question:* Documents were effective *because* they reflected pre-existing social unity, not because they invented it. This raises a policy implication: **Can policy frameworks succeed if they attempt to radically transform a society rather than reflect its values?**
   - *Key sources:* All secondary sources + primary documents; Dr. Ross's framing.

**Writing Guidance (Standing from Week 1):**
- **Answer every part of the prompt.** Don't just address one sub-question.
- **Never merely summarize.** Synthesize sources; probe tensions between authors (e.g., Lutz's covenant theology vs. Fischer's social/cultural focus).
- **Be concise and pithy.** Use "Slow Reading" discipline: write substantively but efficiently. (See [../slow_reading_lists_fletcher_notes.md](../slow_reading_lists_fletcher_notes.md).)
- **Cite ≥4 scholarly sources** in current APA 7 format. You may cite the primary documents *and* secondary sources.
- **Minimum 750 words** (this naturally emerges if you answer all parts thoroughly).

## Instructor Guidance & Standing Lecture Notes

### **Prof. Erik Root's Key Points (from Week 2 Video Lecture):**

**"People Before Parchment"**
- The colonists constituted themselves as distinct political bodies *before* drafting formal documents.
- Legal documents formalized what already existed culturally.
- The legitimacy of early American constitutionalism rested on its reflection of pre-existing social unity, not on its invention of it.

**Four English Subcultures (Fischer's Framework):**
- **Puritans** (Massachusetts Bay): Highly educated, middle-class, valued ordered liberty. Formed covenant theology-based church-state. Hierarchical but not aristocratic.
- **Cavaliers** (Virginia): Aristocratic royalists fleeing English Civil War. Recreated hierarchical agrarian society; enforced brutal social order (Virginia's Laws Divine, Moral and Martial).
- **Quakers** (Delaware Valley/PA): Radical egalitarians. Believed in Inner Light; no denominational hierarchy. William Penn's Charter of Liberties was the most democratic experiment of its era.
- **Scots-Irish** (Appalachian Backcountry): Borderland peoples with clan-based organization. Fiercely independent and localist.

**The Legitimacy Question (Core Policy Implication):**
- A constitution succeeds when it reflects a community's values, not when it attempts to transform the society from the top down.
- This raises a crucial question for contemporary policy: **To what degree can policy frameworks be effective if they attempt to completely reorganize or transform a political community, rather than reflecting its established, organic values?**
- *Example:* Virginia's attempt to impose aristocratic order on a frontier society vs. Pennsylvania's democratic model aligning with Quaker values.

### **Prof. Root's Recurring Guidance (for all discussions):**
1. Answer all parts of the prompt; don't cherry-pick.
2. Avoid superficial engagement. Probe tensions between authors, not just summarize.
3. Use careful, concise prose. Write pithily.
4. Never mistake modern concepts (e.g., "religious liberty," "democracy") for historical meanings. Contextualize.
