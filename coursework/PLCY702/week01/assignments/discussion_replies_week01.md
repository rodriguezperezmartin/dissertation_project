## Reply to Amel

Amel, your post provides a compelling foundation to reflect upon the moral and philosophical origins of constitutionalism. Constitutional government fundamentally depends on whether a society recognizes a higher moral standard that transcends raw state coercion. In classical and Hebraic political history, as McClellan (2000) highlights, the ancient Israelite covenant (berith) established that political rulers are subordinate to a transcendent, divine moral law. When the Israelites demanded an earthly king in 1 Samuel 8, they chose the centralized, coercive power of mortal monarchy over spiritual self-governance which they had previously. Thus this lead to the acceptance of severe burdens of executive overreach, arbitrary taxation, and administrative subjugation from their new rulers. As 19th-century religious leader John Taylor recorded regarding self-governance, enduring order emerges when people are taught correct principles and govern themselves (Taylor, 1851). This insight reflects the warning recorded by Joseph Smith that "it is the nature and disposition of almost all men, as soon as they get a little authority, as they suppose, they will immediately begin to exercise unrighteous dominion" (Doctrine and Covenants 121:39). Because human nature remains inherently prone to self-aggrandizement, internal moral convictions alone are insufficient; they require enforceable institutional architecture.

This institutional tension can be seen in Mexico's constitutional evolution. While the United States anchored its constitutional order in institutional checks and historical continuity (McClellan, 2000), Mexico's framework incorporates a profound structural contradiction. As legal scholar Alejandro Madrazo (2016) observes, Article 39 of the Mexican Constitution codifies a perpetual "right to revolution," declaring that national sovereignty resides essentially and originally in the people, who retain at all times the inalienable right to alter or modify their form of government. Madrazo (2016) argues that this entrenchment of permanent constituent power undermines institutional stability, subordinating the objective rule of law to cyclical political upheavals and factional whims.

When a constitutional system allows transient majorities or ruling political classes to rewrite fundamental law under the banner of popular sovereignty, it compromises what McClellan (2000) identifies as the primary imperative of constitutional design: stability, continuity, and predictability. Without fixed legal boundaries to constrain popular passions and executive maneuvers, a constitution risks degenerating into what Sartori (1962) classifies as a nominal or façade instrument—a political text that outlines state authority without imposing authentic, garantiste restraints on power.

Ultimately, sustainable public policy and constitutional stability requires that provisions for popular sovereignty remains subordinate to permanent institutional boundaries, which will ensure that transient political passions do not dismantle the foundational rule of law.

## Reply to Zaria

Your post provides a compelling foundation to reflect upon various historical states and regimes in world history that embraced the spirit of constitutionalism at heart but failed to establish the structural checks and legal framework needed to protect citizens' liberties while limiting government power. Prior to the ratification of the United States Constitution in 1787, several early American states framed revolutionary constitutions which did not account for all of these critical checks and balances or counterweights (Wood, 1969). Driven by the fear of royal tyranny experienced under the British Crown, framers in states like Pennsylvania established dominant unicameral legislatures with no meaningful executive veto or independent judicial review (McClellan, 2000). While these early constitutionalists were passionately devoted to the spirit republicanism and liberty, their institutional designs demonstrated that moral devotion to freedom cannot endure without the needed structural friction.

James Madison warned in Federalist No. 48 that a legislative assembly operating without checks can swiftly concentrate power in its own hands, producing what Thomas Jefferson termed "elective despotism" (Madison, 1788/2008). McClellan (2000) explains that the American Framers learned through bitter post-Revolutionary experience that moral declarations of rights remain mere "parchment barriers" if institutional checks and balances do not give each branch the constitutional means to resist encroachment (pp. 147–152).

How important it is then for mankind to adhere itself to the Rule of Law - for its power to create stability and order. This institutional principle mirrors the Hebraic legal traditions that influenced early American legal philosophy. In Deuteronomy 4:1–2, Moses instructed the Israelites of the binding nature of the statutes and judgments given to them. He explicitly warned them against adding to or diminishing from the codified law (Deuteronomy 4:1–2, King James Version). Moses emphasized that fundamental law is not an arbitrary tool of mortal discretion to be altered at will. Society derives its stability and inheritance through unwavering adherence to established law rather than the impulsive whims of men.

## Reply to Shannon

Your insights and analysis raise an important point regarding the fragility of constitutionalism—that which a written constitution guarantees very little if those governing authorities who execute the law are not bound by institutional accountability. Your insights are very meaningful in calling out the clear distinction between real constitutional regimes and those with an empty institutional framework.

We can indeed see this tension in contemporary Latin American governance, particularly in Mexico. Articles 6 and 7 of the Mexican Constitution formally establish robust protections for freedom of thought, expression, and digital communication. These were put in place to mirror our Freedom of Speech right and Freedom of the Press. However, recent legislative initiatives—such as the highly contested reforms to Article 403 Bis regarding digital identity and intellectual property (which is often critiqued as "anti-meme" legislation)—demonstrate how easily executive and legislative majorities can draft ambiguous legal language which threatens political sarcasm and digital discord. While many supporters argue such measures target fraud and protect institutional integrity, constitutional legal experts warn that broad punishing liabilities create severe effects on political humor, memes, and public criticism of leadership.

This dynamic confirms Sartori's (1962) warning regarding the danger of façade or nominal constitutional mechanisms. When a government crafts declarations of civil liberties on paper but deploys instruments to penalize citizens for satirizing political figures, the constitution ceases to function as a garantiste "technique of liberty." As Loughlin (2022) notes, modern governance frequently weaponizes administrative and regulatory authority to discipline public discourse, thus gradually neutralizing authentic popular sovereignty under the disguise of statutory legality. Again, your observations from tribal governance resonate here: legitimacy cannot be purchased through the formal trappings of legal documents. It must be demonstrated by institutional willingness to respect dissent and uphold legal limits. This is especially true when political speech challenges those who hold power.
