# Reading Notes & Study Guide: Sartori, "Constitutionalism: A Preliminary Discussion" (1962)

**Purpose:** High-level study notes prepared for Week 1 of PLCY 702, synthesizing Giovanni Sartori's classic article defining "constitutionalism" against the loose, purely descriptive use of "constitution."

**Source:** [../original_src_files/Sartori-ConstitutionalismPreliminaryDiscussion-1962.pdf](../original_src_files/Sartori-ConstitutionalismPreliminaryDiscussion-1962.pdf) (archived copy)

**Full Citation:** Sartori, G. (1962). Constitutionalism: A preliminary discussion. *American Political Science Review, 56*(4), 853–864. https://doi.org/10.2307/1952788

**Note:** This is a study-notes synthesis, not a reproduction of the article's text — written for exam/discussion prep and to stand alongside the Week 1 McClellan and Loughlin summaries. Read the archived PDF directly for exact wording and full argumentation.

## I. The Central Problem: Two Meanings of "Constitution"

Sartori opens by noting that in the 19th century the word "constitution" had a reasonably definite meaning — the one exception being the British constitution, whose ambiguity (no single written document, "fusion" rather than "division" of powers) made it an awkward fit for the concept it supposedly originated. From this puzzle he isolates two competing, incompatible senses of "constitution" that still coexist today:

1. **The *garantiste* (substantive/prescriptive) meaning:** a constitution is a specific "technique of liberty" — a frame of political society, organized through and by law, for the purpose of *restraining* arbitrary power and protecting the rights of those subject to it. This is the meaning the word acquired in America (1776–1787) and in France.
2. **The formal/"cosmic" meaning:** a constitution is simply *any* organizational plan of a state — "the collection of legal and non-legal rules which govern the government," regardless of whether those rules actually limit power. Sartori shows that under this definition, dictatorial regimes (Hitler's Germany, Stalin's USSR) are just as "constitutional" as liberal democracies, because the definition has no restraining content — it is purely descriptive of *any* form.

**Key move:** Sartori argues the formal/cosmic definition is not a neutral, more "scientific" usage — it is a *recent loosening* of the term that empties it of meaning, making "constitution" a deceiving synonym for "organization," "structure," or "political system."

## II. Historical/Etymological Argument

- The Latin *constitutio* originally meant an **enactment** (a law issued by the sovereign) — essentially the opposite of the modern sense.
- Because English medieval lawyers used the term only rarely (unlike their Continental counterparts), "constitution" became a "vacant term" in English — available to be filled with new meaning later.
- The modern, garantiste sense only crystallizes with Bolingbroke and then decisively in **America, 1776–1787** — not in classical antiquity. The French borrowed the modern usage from the Philadelphia Convention, not directly from England.
- Sartori rejects the common claim that Aristotle's *politeia* is the ancestor of "constitution" in its modern sense — this is a **mistranslation**: *politeia* only describes how a polity is patterned (a loose, formal sense), while the modern English term was deliberately coined to mean something much narrower and more prescriptive (limiting arbitrary power). Treating the "any state form" meaning as historically prior is therefore an "optical illusion."

## III. Why the Formal/"Any State Order" Definition Took Hold

- Continental jurists (civil-law tradition) wanted a "universal," rationalistically tidy definition of constitution that would apply to every state, while *simultaneously* retaining the garantiste sense for "truly constitutional" states — an inconsistent but long-tolerated compromise (roughly 19th century to the 1920s).
- The decisive shift toward purely formal, "organizational" definitions (Wheare, Jennings in Britain; also Italian/German jurists under Fascism) was driven less by internal legal logic than by the political climate of the 1920s–30s — a shift from "feeble" (legalitarian) politics to "intense" (warlike) politics, and, in Italy and 1930s Germany, outright political pressure on jurists to adopt neutral, depoliticized definitions that could accommodate authoritarian regimes.

## IV. The "Is" vs. "Ought" Argument — Definitions Are Not Arbitrary Stipulations

- Sartori rejects the idea that "constitution" can simply be stipulated to mean whatever a given author chooses ("this is my definition, take it or leave it").
- His alternative: definitions with a historical referent are **"storehouses of past experience"** shaped by trial-and-error over the practical question *"How can we be governed without being oppressed?"* On this basis, a definition can have genuine truth-value, not just private/conventional validity.
- Because political vocabulary is actively exploited for "double-talk" (e.g., calling any regime "constitutional" to launder its legitimacy), Sartori insists political scientists have a responsibility to say not just "this is what constitution means" but **"this is what it ought to mean"** — a terminological/logical "ought," not an ethical one — in order to prevent the term from being deployed to deceive.

## V. Typology: Real, Nominal, and Facade Constitutions

Building on Karl Loewenstein's framework, Sartori classifies contemporary "constitutions" into three categories:

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Real constitution** | A genuine garantiste constitution: it is actually applied and does restrain power. |
| **Nominal constitution** | Merely an "organizational" document — it accurately/sincerely describes how power actually works, but does not restrain it (a transparent, if illiberal, system). It is not a "dead letter"; it's just irrelevant to the *telos* (purpose) of constitutionalism. |
| **Facade constitution** | Takes the *appearance* of a real constitution but is systematically disregarded in its essential garantiste features — a "trap-constitution" designed to deceive observers about how power actually operates. |

- This typology matters analytically because without the garantiste qualification, the distinction between "a constitution" and "constitutional government" collapses entirely (a government that always acts according to a merely organizational/nominal constitution would, by definition, always be "constitutional" — an empty claim).
- Sartori also addresses the gap between the written and the "living" constitution and the frequent non-enforcement of specific provisions: he is untroubled by gradual evolution/interpretation (the American experience shows written constitutions can endure this), but he treats willful non-enforcement of garantiste provisions as a genuine "delinquency" worth naming — while being indifferent to non-enforcement of merely technical/organizational provisions.

## VI. Sartori's Eight Theses (Summary of the Article's Argument)

1. "Constitution" was re-conceptualized for a specific purpose: to denote a distinctive, unprecedented "technique of liberty," with roots visible as early as Montesquieu's observations of England (c. 1730).
2. This purpose was often obscured by the complexity of the original English model and by British scholars' polemical insistence on their system's uniqueness — but the idea of *limit* is basic to the English, American, and French constitutional models alike.
3. The view that the garantiste meaning was preceded by an older, looser formal meaning lacks historical proof — it rests on a mistranslation of Aristotle's *politeia*.
4. The equivalence "constitution = any state form" is therefore not the *original*, older meaning but a *recent* loosening of the concept.
5. On either account, "constitution" should either be used in its specific garantiste sense, or it is a meaningless/deceiving duplicate of words like "organization," "structure," or "political system."
6. Given this, contemporary "constitutions" can be classified into three types: **real, nominal, and facade**.
7. The existence of nominal constitutions means we must either hold to garantisme, or abandon any meaningful distinction between "constitution" and "constitutional government."
8. Political scientists bear special responsibility for ruling out insincere/deceptive uses of "constitution" — both as a general duty (the observer's language must clarify, not merely mirror, the language of the observed) and because terminological distortion is often deliberately fostered to mislead.

## VII. Closing Argument: Why Political Scientists Should Care

- Sartori notes constitutionalism has not traditionally been central to political science (as distinct from law), and that the "new" behavioral political science of his era — focused on informal process, grassroots groups, and dynamic change — tends to underrate constitutions as static, overly abstract, merely "juridical" artifacts.
- His tentative proposed remedy: approach constitutions through a **"role theory"** lens — studying a constitution's effect on enforcing a desired "role performance" upon power-holders, rather than treating it as a purely formal legal shell.
- Final warning (drawing on the Italian and German experience of the preceding 30–40 years): when a fundamentally political problem like constitutionalism is "depoliticized" by purely neutral/formal legal analysis, the practical effect is not neutrality — it is to clear the way for unscrupulous use of power under the camouflage of a respectable word. **"Politics cannot be taken out of politics."**

## VIII. Connections to McClellan and Loughlin (Week 1 Synthesis)

- **McClellan** treats the American Constitution as the historically tested, successful embodiment of exactly the garantiste "technique of liberty" Sartori describes — limited government secured through structural mechanisms (separation of powers, checks and balances, judicial review) rather than abstract theory.
- **Loughlin** also insists on separating *constitution* (descriptive/institutional) from *constitutionalism* (normative ideology) — paralleling Sartori's constitution/constitutionalism distinction almost exactly — but reaches a very different evaluative conclusion: where Sartori treats the garantiste, power-limiting meaning as the correct and valuable one, Loughlin argues modern constitutionalism's judicial-supremacy turn has become an *anti-political* ideology that displaces, rather than enables, legitimate democratic self-government.
- Useful discussion-thread angle: Sartori's "nominal vs. facade" typology offers a sharper analytical vocabulary for evaluating whether a given country's constitutional text is a *real*, a *nominal*, or a *facade* constitution — directly useful for the Week 1 Discussion prompt on what distinguishes "constitutionalism" from any particular "constitution."
