# Dr. Jason Ross — "The Origins and Nature of Constitutionalism" (Required Viewing, Week 1)

**Speaker:** Dr. Jason Ross, Associate Dean and Associate Professor, Helms School of Government, Liberty University
**Context:** Required Module 1 video for PLCY 702 ("Watch: The Origins and Nature of Constitutionalism"), introducing the course and framing the semester's core question of how constitutionalism emerged and what it means.

## Executive Summary

Dr. Ross opens by justifying why a Founding Era/Constitution course matters for doctoral public policy study: every U.S. public policy issue today is shaped — directly or indirectly, formally or informally — by constitutional provisions, principles (republicanism, federalism), institutional structures (legislative/executive/judicial), and the *conflict* deliberately built between them (Madison's Federalist 51 separation-of-powers/checks-and-balances design, plus federal-state conflict). These institutional conflicts are not a design flaw; they are the strategy, and policy scholars need to track how conflicting constitutional interpretations and inter-branch/inter-level disputes shape policy outcomes in their own research area.

He then situates the Constitution within America's unique "constitutional political culture" at the Founding: a profoundly (for its time) democratic but still skeptical-of-pure-democracy people, with a broader franchise than any other nation (60–90% of white males could vote vs. ~15% in England), shaped overwhelmingly by dissenting Protestantism and by several distinct English regional subcultures transplanted to North America.

The lecture's core content traces the **origins and nature of constitutionalism** through three lenses:
1. **Definitions** — contrasts the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's definition (government legally limited in its powers, with legitimacy depending on observing those limits) against Martin Loughlin's *Against Constitutionalism* definition (constitutionalism as a theory of the role/standing/telos of the distinctly modern "documentary constitution," resting on two pillars: limited power and representation).
2. **Ancient constitutionalism** — Aristotle's classification of regimes by *who rules* (one/few/many) and *for what end* (private interest vs. common good), yielding six regime types, with "constitutional government" (rule by the many for the common good) being so rare it scarcely had a name.
3. **English constitutionalism** — the common law tradition (post-1066 Norman courts, judicial precedent), the Magna Carta (1215) as a practical noble reaction against an unpopular king rather than a theoretical document, its "free men"/due-process/trial-by-peers principles that echo directly in the 5th, 7th, and 14th Amendments, and the "King in Parliament" mixed-regime solution (King = the one, Lords = the few, Commons = the many).

He then isolates what makes **American** constitutionalism historically unprecedented: (1) no class/rank/order-based distribution of rule (unlike ancient/English models) — even though 18th-century American society excluded women and enslaved Africans from political rights, it was still the most democratic society that had existed; and (2) the Constitution is **written down**, binding future generations to original intent while also letting its textual principles (liberty, due process) be extended beyond the particular people/history that produced them — which is exactly the argument abolitionists and African Americans used to claim constitutional principles applied to them too, later vindicated in the 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments.

**Closing argument:** Since the Founding, the *written* constitution has gone from an idiosyncratic practice of rebellious English colonists to the mandatory global standard of political legitimacy (all 193 UN-recognized states now have one) — "constitutionalism is America's greatest export." But Dr. Ross poses the semester's open question: **has the world exported the *form* of written constitutionalism without exporting the underlying *political culture* (Protestant moral culture, fear of tyranny, local self-government) that originally animated it?** This tension — between constitutionalism's universal modern norms and the particular culture/history that produced them — is framed as the subtext running through the entire course.

## Relevance to Week 1 Readings/Discussion
This lecture is the connective thread for the Week 1 McClellan, Loughlin, and Sartori readings:
- It previews **McClellan's** English-origins argument (common law, Magna Carta) and the written-vs-unwritten constitution distinction.
- It directly summarizes **Loughlin's** two-pillars definition (limited powers + representation) and his insistence that these are modern, largely American inventions — material directly usable in the Discussion thread's prompt on what distinguishes "constitutionalism" from any particular "constitution."
- It supplies the **Aristotelian typology** (one/few/many; private interest vs. common good) that both McClellan and Sartori reference, and that Sartori implicitly measures the Soviet "constitution" against.
- The closing "form without culture" question is a strong original discussion angle not explicitly raised by any of the three assigned readings — worth citing directly in the thread.

## Full Transcript

Welcome to Policy 702, The Founding Era, and the Constitution. I'm Dr. Jason Ross, Associate Dean and Associate Professor in the Helm School of Government. I want to begin by talking about why a course on the founding era and the Constitution is relevant to doctoral study in public policy.

First, in the United States today, there is no public policy issue that's not framed or directly controlled by some constitutional provision or principle that either authorizes or limits the use of political power. These powers or limits on them may have changed over time. Those changes may have been formal due to constitutional amendments, interpretations, or legislation. Or they may have been informal due to precedents and practices that have long been settled. Still, every area of American public policy is influenced by constitutional structures and principles like republicanism and federalism.

Every area of public policy is shaped by constitutional institutions, including the legislative branch, executive branch and judicial branch, and by the powers granted to them. More importantly, our public policies are shaped by the conflict between these institutions. This conflict is not an oversight of our constitutional system. Instead, a separation of powers with a system of checks and balances was built into our constitutional system, as James Madison described in his famous 51st Federalist essay, as a way of guarding against the concentration of power in any one branch of government. Likewise, a conflict between federal and state governments was built into the system at specific moments in American politics. These conflicts may seem to indicate some failure of the Constitution, but it's best for us to evaluate the effectiveness of this strategy of allowing one center of power to check another over time.

As public policy scholars, we want to be aware about how these conflicts between and among varying interpretations of the Constitution, between and among varying branches of the federal government and between the federal and state governments influenced the way that public policy is studied and made in the area of our research interest, also in the United States.

Today, there is no public policy issue that is not heavily influenced by what we might call our constitutional political culture. We should all remember that the United States Constitution was drafted and ratified at a moment in time by the peoples of 13 states that while free and independent, broadly shared a common political culture. This political culture feared tyranny and valued local self government. This political culture was more profoundly democratic than any which had existed to that point in human history. Still, Americans of this time viewed democracy with skepticism as little better than mob rule. While the proportion of Americans who could exercise political rights, like the right to vote, was far greater than in any other nation in the world at that time, they still limited the franchise and other political rights to white men. At the time of the founding, the states made laws regarding who could vote — somewhere 60–90% of white males could vote in the states at this time. In England, that number was around 15%. Voting was scarcely known anywhere else in the world.

The political culture of their time was overwhelmingly shaped by dissenting Protestantism. This lent itself to a general wariness of any religious establishment, but still assumed a very broad embrace of traditional Christian doctrine and a very deep connection to the truths and moral teachings of scripture from both Old and New Testaments. Finally, this political culture was overwhelmingly influenced by several English subcultures, each of which had departed the mother country at some point to settle in a different region of North America, transplanting English political traditions that had been filtered through their own particular cultural beliefs and practices. We will come back to many of these points in future modules.

Beyond the significance of the United States Constitution culture out of which it came, this course will also frame the Constitution within the broader history of the development of constitutionalism. After all, we should be aware that during the American founding era, the unwritten British constitution was widely celebrated by leading political thinkers. But Americans were the only ones to have written constitutions. In 1776, after the Declaration of Independence, eight of the free and independent American states adopted their own constitutions. By 1780, all of the American states, with the exception of Rhode Island, had adopted new written constitutions. When the United States Constitution was ratified in 1788, there were 14 written constitutions worldwide — all of them were in the United States. Today, the United Nations officially recognizes 193 states, each of which has a written constitution. Since the American founding, it has become clear that no nation can claim to be seen as legitimate, whether in the eyes of its citizens or in international law, if it does not have a written constitution.

Constitutionalism is America's greatest export. But while it is clear that we have exported the form of written constitutionalism, we may not have exported the political culture out of which American written constitutionalism was born. Can the form of constitutionalism function without the animating spirit of a constitutional culture?

That preface to the course, I want to turn now to a discussion of the origins and nature of constitutionalism. Let's start by trying to define the concept. It may seem simple to define a concept like constitutionalism, but one of the things you will learn as a scholar is that it can be deceptively difficult to define terms in a way that allows for a reliable analysis.

One possible definition for constitutionalism is provided by the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. Constitutionalism is the idea, often associated with the political theories of John Locke and the founders of the American Republic, that government can and should be legally limited in its powers, and that its authority or legitimacy depends on its observing these limitations. This definition prompts a few helpful observations. First, it points to the empirical or factual issues that constitutional lawyers or political scientists might address about the origins and extent of government's powers. It also points to the normative or moral issues that citizens or political philosophers might address about what makes those powers legitimate.

This definition also suggests a few questions. For example, to what degree should a people be bound by a constitution they themselves did not establish or consent to? Also, if a people is bound to a constitution to which they do not consent, and which they find illegitimate, should the constitution itself be deemed illegitimate, or the acts of the people which contravene it? Finally, as a factual matter, can constitutions effectively or legitimately limit government?

Another possible definition is from the recent book, *Against Constitutionalism*, by Martin Loughlin of the London School of Economics. He speaks of constitutionalism as a theory concerning the role, standing, appropriate institutional form and telos, or end, of a purely modern invention — the documentary constitution. He goes on to argue that the modern invention of the documentary constitution rests on two pillars. One is the limiting of powers that we saw in the previous definition. The other is that of representation. This means that people do not exercise power directly, but indirectly, by individuals elected or appointed for the purpose.

These features of constitutionalism are all quite familiar to us and may be so obvious that they might not seem worth mentioning. But Loughlin reminds us that they are all modern inventions. And if they are not entirely American inventions, they were primarily developed through their application in American constitutional practice. Even more fundamentally than this, Loughlin touches on what is the most obvious, but also most underappreciated feature of modern constitutionalism — that constitutions are written down. Again, Americans were the exceptions in world history in writing down their constitutions during the late 18th century. But today, no state can be considered legitimate without a written constitution.

We can start to appreciate the profound impact of this feature of modern constitutionalism when we compare it against the unwritten constitutional forms that were known in the ancient and medieval English world. Some scholars draw a sharp distinction between ancient and modern forms of constitutionalism. Other scholars contend that constitutionalism is a purely modern concept unrelated to ancient constitutions. What cannot be disputed is that ancient societies were constituted in some way.

Aristotle said simply enough that a constitution is a form of organization of the inhabitants of a state. He also said that the word constitution means the same as government. Beyond this, Aristotle gave the classic definition of the various forms of government, or constitution, known to the ancient world to define the way a political society was constituted. Aristotle asked two questions. The first question was, what class of society rules? By this, Aristotle meant, did one supreme or powerful citizen rule? Did a small class rule, preeminent in property or in virtue? Or did the many rule? The other question he asked was, to what end do the rulers rule? Do they rule in their own private interests, or do they rule for the common good of all?

From this, Aristotle categorized six types of regime. There was rule by the one, whether in monarchy or tyranny. There was rule by the few, whether by a noble aristocracy or a selfish oligarchy. And finally, there was rule by the many — he viewed rule by the many in its own interest as a vicious form of government, which he called democracy. His view of democracy, which was also shared by virtually all throughout history, including the American founding generation, was generally negative. It's worth considering why we moderns depart so sharply from the judgments of all who preceded us. On this point, Aristotle also spoke of a form of government in which the many ruled in the interest of all — it was so rare that it scarcely had a name. Aristotle simply referred to it as constitutional government. Aristotle's classic view of constitutions persisted well into the Middle Ages and influenced the emergence of English ideas of constitutionalism.

Let's now look at English constitutionalism, which had a much more direct and profound influence on the emergence of American constitutionalism. The first thing to note about English constitutionalism is that it is the product of particular historical developments of a particular people. One historical dimension of English constitutionalism is the importance of the common law. This was established after the Norman conquest of England in 1066. England's new rulers established a system of courts. The body of judicial decisions made by these courts over time were treated as precedents, having the force of law. Their authority as precedents was common from one part of the realm to others. Much of English political culture and practice is not derived from constitutional conventions, or statutes adopted by legislative assemblies, but by the common law.

Another vital historical development in English constitutionalism was the adoption of the Magna Carta in 1215. It's easy for us 800 years on to misunderstand the circumstances and significance of Magna Carta. This was not a theoretical document — it was the product of English nobles, fed up with the taxes, military service, and harsh penalties imposed by an unpopular king. We see in Magna Carta a few political principles asserted in England for the first time, which remained a part of English political culture and which became key principles of American constitutionalism. These include the assertion that even English subjects must be considered as free men. We will see this principle asserted again in the Declaration of Independence, when Americans denounce King George the Third as a tyrant, not fit to be the ruler of a free people.

Related, Magna Carta asserts no freeman shall be taken, imprisoned, deceased, outlawed, banished, or in any way destroyed, nor will we proceed against or prosecute him except by the lawful judgment of his peers or by the law of the land. We see these principles reasserted in the Fifth Amendment, which claims that no person shall be deprived of life, liberty, or property without due process of law. They're also evident in the Seventh Amendment, which asserts the right of trial by jury. Finally, they're reasserted in the 14th Amendment, which specifies that no state can deprive any person of life, liberty, or property without due process of law, nor deny to any person the equal protection of the laws.

Conflict between king and people remained an important theme in English politics during its turbulent 17th century. England arrived at a constitutional solution intended to address this conflict. This was the notion of the king in parliament. Similar to the classical approach detailed by Aristotle, the English solution identified the one, the few, and the many. Unlike the classical approach, the English mixed regime gave each of these orders of society a share in rule. The King represented rule by one preeminent individual. The House of Lords allowed the landed aristocracy to rule, and the House of Commons allowed for a degree of popular representation.

Now that we know a little bit about constitutionalism of the ancient and English varieties, let's underscore some differences between them and American constitutionalism. These differences point to certain problems in American constitutionalism. First, American constitutionalism is not based on an arrangement of classes, ranks, or orders of society the way ancient or medieval constitutionalism was. That we look back now and see American society during the founding era as being profoundly undemocratic — because it did not grant political rights to women or enslaved Africans — it was at that time the most profoundly democratic society that had ever existed in human history. There was no distinction in American constitutions or government between the privileges or share in rule granted to the one, the few and the many, because there was no distinction in American society between the one, the few and the many.

Also, while ancient and English constitutions were the products of historical developments of particular peoples, and therefore both time-bound and culture-bound, American Constitutions are written down. This means that, on the one hand, American Constitutions can be seen to bind subsequent generations to the intent of the generation that drafted and ratified those documents. On the other hand, this means that while the specific features of American Constitutions were informed by the particular historical developments of particular peoples, the constitutional text themselves can apply outside of the specific history and tradition of a particular people. This suggests how African Americans, along with abolitionists and other opponents of slavery, could argue that constitutional principles of liberty, individual rights, and due process of law necessarily apply to blacks as well as whites. These arguments ultimately prevailed in the view of the Constitution that was established in the 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments.

As we close this first lecture, I want to underscore a few critical points about the United States Constitution and about constitutionalism. Both are marked by the modern feature of having been written down. Because the United States founding documents were written down by an overwhelmingly Protestant people who took their authority from scripture, they have come to be seen as what one leading scholar has called American scripture. This has led to very intense debates in American politics and scholarship about what the founders may or may not have intended.

Finally, the notion of a written constitution was once the idiosyncratic practice of certain English peoples who departed against British imperial rule from their small outposts at the edge of the known world. Now it is the required political practice of any state seeking to be recognized as legitimate. This has required constitutionalism to change in ways that make its norms and expectations applicable to any and all people from any and all places or origins. The norms and ideals of constitutionalism are in tension with, maybe even in direct conflict with, the distinct interests, cultures, or histories of the peoples who introduced the idea of written constitutions to the world. This conflict is a subtext of all constitutional debates that occur today. Please keep it in mind as we proceed through this course. Thank you.
