What are some of the features of modern constitutionalism that may account for it having become, in effect, the only form of government recognized as legitimate?*

## What Political Scientists Mean by Constitutionalism

When political scientists speak of constitutionalism, they do not refer merely to a physical parchment or legal text, but to a distinct normative political philosophy. This philosophy is rooted in the conviction that political power is legitimate only when it is structurally limited—that is, when government is bound by law and subjected to institutional constraints in order to protect human liberties and prevent arbitrary tyranny (Loughlin, 2022; Sartori, 1962). While a written constitution serves as an organizational blueprint allocating government offices, constitutionalism is the underlying principle that actually binds rulers to the law—what Charles McIlwain famously termed a "legal limitation on government" (Loughlin, 2022; McClellan, 2000). Much like Adam Smith's "invisible hand" operating within economic markets, constitutionalism works behind the scenes to establish "ordered liberty" (McClellan, 2000). This equilibrium ensures that the state maintains sufficient order to prevent anarchy, while enforceable legal boundaries prevent that order from degenerating into tyranny.

## Constitutionalism as Distinct from Particular Constitutions

Distinguishing constitutionalism from particular constitutions is essential because possessing a written document does not mean a regime practices limited government (Sartori, 1962). Sartori (1962) warns that adopting a purely formal, "cosmic" definition—where a constitution is merely "the collection of legal and non-legal rules which govern the government" (p. 853)—strips the term of all analytical rigor. Under such a relativistic definition, authoritarian regimes and dictatorships would qualify as constitutional systems simply because they operate through structured state apparatuses (Sartori, 1962, pp. 858–860). Modern regimes such as Venezuela illustrate this distinction: the state possesses a detailed, written constitution, yet authorities routinely bypass institutional checks and ignore its core principles in practice.

To restore conceptual rigor, Sartori (1962) argues that the authentic historical definition of a constitution is garantiste: a specific "technique of liberty" designed to restrain arbitrary authority and safeguard individual rights (pp. 854–855). Sartori classifies constitutional texts into three distinct categories:

- **Real Constitutions:** Substantive frameworks that successfully bind the government to legal limits and ensure civil liberties are safeguarded in practice (Sartori, 1962, p. 861).
- **Nominal Constitutions:** Documents that accurately describe how state power is organized and exercised, but contain no operational limits or restraining checks on authority (Sartori, 1962, p. 861).
- **Façade Constitutions:** Documents that contain all the nominal checks, balances, and declarations of individual rights, but whose guarantees are systematically disregarded and violated by those in power (Sartori, 1962, p. 861). Modern Venezuela serves as a clear example that calls out this case. In 1999 Venezuela ratified their constitution. Though it articulates extensive democratic rights and the separation of powers, the regime bypassed institutional checks and subjugated the judiciary, thus reducing or nullifying its constitution as a simple decorated facade used to legitimize governance (Brewer-Carías, 2010; Corrales, 2015).

This distinction is illuminated in McClellan's (2000) historical analysis of the American constitutional founding. Specific constitutional documents may be drafted, amended, or replaced across nations, but the American constitutional order has endured because it is grounded in the deep Anglo-American tradition of the common law, Magna Carta (1215), and institutional checks and balances developed through centuries of historical experience rather than abstract speculation (McClellan, 2000, pp. 12–25).

## Features Accounting for the Global Legitimacy of Modern Constitutionalism

Modern constitutionalism has emerged as the sole universally recognized form of legitimate governance due to three structural features detailed by Loughlin (2022):

- **Rule by Text and Documentary Supremacy:** It requires a single written documentary artifact drafted in the name of the sovereign people, establishing a visible lex superior that defines the legal boundaries of state power (Loughlin, 2022, pp. 6–9).
- **Representative Institutional Architecture:** As James Madison articulated in Federalist No. 10 and No. 63, modern constitutionalism reconciles popular sovereignty with systemic stability by filtering factional passions through representative delegates while enforcing institutional separation of powers (Loughlin, 2022, pp. 10–13).
- **Universal Codification of Rights:** Governments today secure domestic and international legitimacy by writing human rights directly into supreme law, transforming moral aspirations into enforceable legal rules (Loughlin, 2022, pp. 14–16). Beyond proclaiming abstract principles of justice or dignity, states are to codify these protections so that independent courts can enforce them. This universal codification provides a shared, internationally recognized political grammar. This is needed because it helps to supersede religious or ideological divisions thus establishing legitimacy through the universal language of fundamental rights, due process, and equality before the law.

Ultimately, these structural features account for what is recognized today as a legitimate constitutional order and framework. The American Framing generation indeed established a new pioneering constitutional architecture that balances both human liberties with institutional restraint, providing an enduring model for other nations seeking to secure fundamental rights and self-government under the rule of law.
