## Overview (Instructor's Module Introduction, Verbatim)
In this module you will evaluate different definitions of the concept of constitutionalism. You will explore the historical origins and precedents of constitutionalism.

Be sure to complete all Introduction, Learn, and Apply items in Module 1: Week 1 - The Origins and Nature of Constitutionalism by 11:59 p.m. (ET) on the assigned due date. Use the Modules link on the left side of the screen at any time to view your progress in the course.

## Learning Outcomes (Instructor's Official List, Module 1)
Upon successful completion of this module, you will be able to:
1. Define constitutionalism.
2. Distinguish between empirical and normative uses of the term constitutionalism.
3. Evaluate the legitimacy of constitutionalism.

The author asserts much that is questionable about the U.S. Constitution, but you should think about his relevant point that culture is also a form of Constitution--that is a people are representative of what we might call the city or πολις. If there are a people who no connection to anything other than their own culture, then what happens to the general health of politics? Populism is a reasonable one here--it is a reaction to vast and deep evil/corruption. Doubt become universal, and hence people are more willing to be led--it is not called TEL-A-Vision for nothing!

Laughlin only hints at all this, and he is not seemingly happy with constitutions. give him a serious read, and think what are the implications, then test him with what you see around you today.

## Instructor's Reading Commentary — Discussion Prep Angles
Prof. Root's weekly announcement above supplies specific interpretive angles worth citing/engaging directly in the Discussion thread and replies:
- **McClellan:** "Understand authors as they understand themselves" is Prof. Root's standing interpretive rule for this course — apply it to all three authors. McClellan's point that *culture is itself a form of constitution* (a people as the πολις/city) raises the question of what happens to political health when a people lose connection to anything beyond their own culture, or when new arrivals lack the civic/political understanding of the country they join. Also: Constitutions (per both McClellan and Publius/Federalist) are "mere paper" without a people who embody/enforce them. The U.S. Constitution is *not* simply British — it is written, and paired with the Declaration — even though English common law/institutional history deeply influenced it; don't skim the English primary-source material in the second McClellan reading (pp. 63–88).
- **Loughlin:** Prof. Root frames America as possibly "post-constitutional." He pushes back on Loughlin's dismissal of populism as merely a symptom of constitutionalism's decline — arguing current U.S. populism is instead a reasonable reaction to perceived oligarchy/corruption and a two-tiered justice system — while agreeing with Loughlin's point about a globalist tendency (he references the Club of Rome) to subordinate national constitutions to supranational/technocratic control. Also raises Marshall McLuhan's media theory (broadcast "unreality," universalized doubt) as a mechanism of modern, tech-enabled tyranny distinct from ancient tyranny.
- **Sartori:** Prof. Root challenges Sartori's claim that the Soviet Constitution "existed" independent of Stalin's will by invoking the USSR's ~20 million dead, and questions whether a "People's Republic" can really be "of the people" or a "republic" in any meaningful sense. He frames Sartori as a modern academic caught within his own modernity's assumptions, suggesting Aristotle may hold the better answer that Sartori can't quite reach.
