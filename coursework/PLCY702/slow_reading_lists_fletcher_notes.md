## Core Idea

The term "slow reading" comes from Nietzsche's preface to *Daybreak*, where he calls himself "a teacher of slow reading" — reading that is deliberate, patient (*lento*), and resistant to the "hurry" of an age that wants to "get everything done" with every book. Fletcher (a philosophy professor) built his own teaching practice around this idea for decades.

Fletcher's key distinction: **slow reading does not start with a special way of reading — it starts with *stopping* and *turning around*.** Most of us read the way we drive on a highway: speeding toward a destination (a conclusion, an opinion, an assignment to finish), half-noticing that something along the way didn't look right, but not stopping to check. Slow reading begins when you stop and go back to actually look at what you passed.

## Fletcher's Core Argument: We Replace the Author's Words with Our Own Meaning Too Quickly

- **The test:** Read a sentence aloud to a group and ask them to repeat it back word-for-word. Almost nobody does. Instead, people report what they think the sentence *meant* — in their own words. We are "preoccupied with deciding what the sentences we read and hear MEAN to us," and whether we agree or disagree, before we even register what the text actually *says*.
- **The risk:** This habit lets a reader "impose their own meanings on the text" rather than engaging with what the author was actually trying to say — the opposite of genuine intellectual engagement.
- **Illustrative anecdote:** A blind seven-year-old musical prodigy screams that the piano is out of tune; her mother, hearing distress, assumes she's hungry and offers food. The mother imposed her own "kitchen listening" framework on her daughter's words instead of asking what she actually meant — until the daughter corrected her. Fletcher's point: most readers behave like the mother, substituting their own frame for the author's intent, and rarely get corrected because the "author" (the text) usually can't talk back — unless we deliberately pause to ask what it is actually saying.

## The "Assume the Author Is God" Teaching Device

Fletcher's central pedagogical technique: when starting a course on a major text, he tells students to **assume the text was written by God** — not as a theological claim, but as a discipline:
- If the author cannot be wrong, then when you hit a passage that seems confused, contradictory, or wrong, you cannot blame the author — you are forced to first suspect your own ignorance or misreading.
- This reverses the default move, which is to blame the author ("that guy was confused," "he's just making fallacious arguments") rather than treat a confusing passage as **an opportunity to learn** something you don't yet understand.
- He's explicit that this is a *working hypothesis*, not a claim you must ultimately accept — by the time you get around to deciding the author might really have been mistaken, you will have fully understood the *questions* the author meant to raise. Skipping that step means you risk never understanding the actual question at all.

## Why This Matters (Fletcher's Closing Argument)

- Dismissing "authorial intent" as unknowable or irrelevant and interpreting texts purely "based on our own ideas" is, in ordinary conversation, recognized as absurd and disrespectful — refusing to accept what a living speaker says they meant. Applying that same disrespect to written authors (dead or alive) degrades the practice of reading into a kind of monologue with yourself.
- The deeper aim of slow reading is not just getting the "right" interpretation — it's learning to have a genuine **conversation** with serious thinkers (including thinkers who are dead), which requires the same listening-before-speaking discipline as a respectful conversation with a living person.

## Direct Application to PLCY 702 Discussions

Prof. Root's standing guidance (paired with his recommendation of Fletcher's essay) for the Discussion assignments:
1. **Answer all the questions asked, and develop your argument** — don't just gesture at a prompt.
2. **Write concisely and favor pithy, precise statements** — he points to Strunk (*The Elements of Style*) for help with this.
3. **Do not merely regurgitate the readings** — summarizing McClellan/Loughlin/Sartori back to the professor is not the assignment. Think critically about what the authors actually argue and where they might be wrong, incomplete, or in tension with each other.
4. **Example probe he gave:** *What makes government legitimate?* The Declaration of Independence is **not** among this week's assigned readings, yet the Founders had a specific, identifiable theory of government legitimacy (consent of the governed, unalienable rights, the right to alter/abolish government) — and this is not exactly the same thing Alexis de Tocqueville argues in *Democracy in America* (a required course text). Students are encouraged to "suss that out" — i.e., work out for themselves where the Founders' Declaration-based legitimacy theory and Tocqueville's view of democratic legitimacy actually diverge — rather than treating all "Founding-era" or "early American" thought as a single undifferentiated point of view.
5. **Fletcher's slow-reading discipline is the practical method for doing #3 and #4 well** — approach McClellan, Loughlin, and Sartori (and later Tocqueville) by first finding out exactly what each author is actually saying and intends, before deciding whether you agree, rather than skimming for a thesis to argue with.
