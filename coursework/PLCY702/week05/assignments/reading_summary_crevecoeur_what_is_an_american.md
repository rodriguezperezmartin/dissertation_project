## I. Executive Summary

Crèvecoeur, writing as a fictional Pennsylvania farmer, answers the question "What is an American?" by describing a social process rather than a single national character. Europeans arrive, are absorbed into a country with few fixed hierarchies, and are transformed into Americans. Three claims carry the argument: (1) American society is organized around property-owning freeholders who are not bound to lords; (2) the new settler, once employed and treated as a person, gains a sense of dignity that Europe denied him; and (3) religious and national distinctions weaken into an American identity. Crèvecoeur also draws a sharp contrast between settled, orderly communities and the frontier, where he describes lawlessness and degeneration. For Week 5, the letter is the clearest eyewitness statement of the "equality" that Europeans reported. It is social, economic, and experiential, and it is not yet a political principle.

## II. Structure of the Letter

1. **Frontier gradient (opening section).** Crèvecoeur moves from the coast inland. He describes settlements becoming less religious and less refined as they approach the frontier. He presents the back-settlers as a "forlorn hope" who clear the land and are later displaced by "more industrious people."
2. **Provincial variation.** Each colony develops its own character from government, climate, and husbandry. Europeans "become, in the course of a few generations, not only Americans in general, but either Pennsylvanians, Virginians, or provincials."
3. **Religious indifference.** Sects settle near each other, but mixed neighborhoods gradually weaken religious zeal. Crèvecoeur presents this as a result of freedom of worship, not persecution.
4. **The freeholder.** The "true American freeholder" is the central figure. He owns the land he works, belongs to a government he helps form, and writes his own laws through representatives. Europe, by contrast, has "hardly any other distinctions but lords and tenants."
5. **The transformation of the immigrant.** Crèvecoeur narrates an emigrant's path from wage laborer to landowner. The emigrant is fed at his employer's table, is treated "as a member of the family," and "feels himself a man, because he is treated as such."
6. **National comparison.** Germans, Scots, and Irish are compared. Germans and Scots tend to succeed, and the Irish are described as less prosperous. Crèvecoeur describes this as a question of habits, not inherent nature.
7. **Closing exemplar.** A Scottish settler's progress is narrated, and the letter ends with a personified "great parent" who offers "ease and independence" to the industrious European.

## III. Key Themes Relevant to Equality

- **Equality of condition, not of rank.** Crèvecoeur does not claim that Americans are equal in wealth or talent. He claims that "there is room for every body in America." The equality he describes is access: anyone who is "sober, honest, and industrious" can obtain land, wages, and standing.
- **Dissolving of European hierarchy.** The emigrant "instead of being employed by a haughty person, … finds himself with his equal." Social deference is replaced by a relationship of reciprocity in the workplace and household.
- **Dignity through recognition.** The most striking passage shows the emigrant moving "from a servant to the rank of a master" and from "the slave of some despotic prince" to "a free man." Crèvecoeur calls this a "resurrection," which indicates that equality is experienced as a felt social status rather than a legal claim.
- **Property as the basis of citizenship.** Freehold land is what makes a man an American. Crèvecoeur says the emigrant "is now an American, a Pennsylvanian, an English subject" once he holds the deed. Political membership follows from ownership.
- **Conditional and moral equality.** Access is open, but only to those who work. Crèvecoeur repeatedly distinguishes the "industrious" from the "idle," the "drunken," and the "litigious." The equality is real and also conditional on character.
- **Religious and national pluralism.** Sects and nations are "mixed," and European identities are "lost." This produces a common American identity out of difference.

## IV. Points of Tension for the Week 5 Discussion

- **Equality versus hierarchy in the frontier passages.** Crèvecoeur's frontier is violent and unequal in its own way, shaped by lawlessness and relations with Native peoples. He attributes the 1774 Indian hostilities against Virginians to the abuses of frontier traders, who "trade with them" and "defraud the Indians."
- **Who counts as the "American."** The letter describes European immigrants as its subject. Enslaved Africans and Native peoples are almost absent from the account of who becomes an American, except as objects of frontier violence.
- **Gratitude to the crown.** Crèvecoeur ends with thanks to "God bless the king, and William Penn" and "that insular government." This complicates a later reading of the letter as an anti-monarchical text. It is pre-Revolutionary in tone, and the letter was first published in 1782, during the war.

## V. Connection to Other Week 5 Readings

- **Tocqueville** (Vol. II, Part II) describes American equality of condition in similar terms. Compare Crèvecoeur's lived account with Tocqueville's analysis of democratic social habits.
- **Jefferson's Declaration draft** uses "all men are created equal" as a political principle. Crèvecoeur shows the social experience that could give that phrase meaning for an immigrant.
- **Fischer's regional cultures** explain the variation Crèvecoeur describes across provinces. Crèvecoeur's account of Scots, Germans, and Irish fits Fischer's "folkways" framework.

## VI. Study Questions

1. What does Crèvecoeur mean when he says the immigrant "feels himself a man, because he is treated as such"?
2. Is Crèvecoeur's equality a matter of condition, law, or recognition? How would you tell the difference from the text?
3. How does property change the political identity of the immigrant in his account?
4. What does the frontier passage add to his claim that Europeans become Americans?
5. How does Crèvecoeur's account of Native peoples and enslaved people limit his "American" category?
