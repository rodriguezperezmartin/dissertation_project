## I. Executive Summary

Boulton argues that Jefferson's "paradox" is real but has been poorly explained. The Declaration's phrase "all men are created equal" was abstract and open to many meanings. Jefferson's early draft grounded equality in an "equal creation," a natural-law idea he later dropped. In *Notes on the State of Virginia* (1781–82), he tried to ground the Declaration's principles in empirical science, and that effort produced a racial hierarchy. Boulton argues that Jefferson's racial thinking was not simply a defense of slavery; Jefferson also held a lifelong antislavery position. Boulton's larger claim is that the idea of race itself was being invented in the late eighteenth century, and that the natural-law idea of equality and the new racial categories developed together.

---

## II. The Ambiguity of "Equality" (pp. 470–472)

**Thesis.** The Declaration's equality principle is genuinely ambiguous, and Jefferson may have intended more than critics assume.

**Key mechanisms.**
- *Competing readings:* some historians read Jefferson's equality as only equal opportunity to compete in the market, and others as special privileges for white male property owners. Boulton says these readings are "perhaps only partly correct." Greene's line expresses the first reading: "Every man was to have an equal opportunity to become less equal" (p. 471).
- *The "equal creation" phrase:* Jefferson's draft grounded rights in "equal creation," language taken from Locke's *Second Treatise*. Jefferson omitted it from the final text. His 1821 autobiography details the draft's changes but leaves out this phrase (p. 472).
- *Tocqueville on abstraction:* Boulton quotes Tocqueville's remark that "an abstract term [such as equality] is like a box with a false bottom," which explains why democracies keep the word (p. 472). The source is Tocqueville's chapter on how democracy changed the English language, Vol. II.

**Constitutional takeaway.** The ambiguity of "equality" was part of its power. It could support almost any position.

---

## III. *Notes on the State of Virginia* and the Racial Argument (pp. 472–477)

**Thesis.** Jefferson tried to ground equality in scientific proof, and in doing so he produced a racial hierarchy.

**Key mechanisms.**
- *Jefferson's descriptions:* Boulton quotes Jefferson on the "immoveable veil of black" and on blacks as "in reason much inferior" and "dull, tasteless, and anomalous" (pp. 472–473).
- *Not only contemporary opinion:* Boulton argues that Jefferson participated in a cosmopolitan science of man (Newton, Bacon, Locke), so "presentism" does not fully explain his views (p. 473).
- *Antislavery record:* Boulton lists Jefferson's antislavery acts: a resolution easing manumission, a gradual-emancipation plan, the Northwest exclusion of slavery, and pressing for the 1808 slave-trade ban. He argues that Jefferson's antislavery commitments were consistent throughout his life (pp. 473–475).
- *Race as a new category:* Boulton argues that the modern idea of race was constructed in the late eighteenth century. Before then, people were grouped by nation, religion, and custom. The natural-law tradition that asserted equality also raised the importance of physical distinctions, which then became "race" (pp. 476–477).

**Constitutional takeaway.** The Revolution turned slavery into the central metaphor for political evil, and the new idea of race then became the justification for slavery.

---

## IV. Slavery, Freedom, and the "New American" (pp. 475–486)

**Thesis.** Slavery and freedom became opposites in the Revolutionary language, and race was needed to justify slavery after freedom became a universal right.

**Key mechanisms.**
- *Slavery as the political evil:* Washington, Dickinson, Quincy, and Adams all used slavery as the image of colonial dependence (pp. 475–476).
- *Rhetoric of race as justification:* once freedom was universal in theory, slavery needed a new justification, and race provided it (p. 476).
- *Crèvecoeur and Jefferson:* Boulton argues that texts such as Crèvecoeur's *Letters* and Jefferson's *Notes* invented an ideal "New American," a free farmer of mixed European stock opposed to hierarchy, dependency, and slavery. He calls this ideal a prerequisite for American national identity (p. 486).

**Constitutional takeaway.** The compromises over slavery and race in the Constitution were built on the same intellectual foundation as the ideal of the "New American."

---

## V. Conclusion: The Language of Myth (pp. 486–487)

Boulton argues that Americans have repeatedly resolved conflicts between sections, parties, and classes by agreeing on racial distinctions, and that the story of race and slavery "deserves to be told... in the language of myth." The idea of race has expressed the conflicting passions of modern society, and Americans have created a narrative in which the darker self mirrors their greatest hopes and fears.
