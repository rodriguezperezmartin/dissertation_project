## Module 5 Overview (Canvas Module Introduction)

**Overview**

Many argue the Constitution should be subordinated to the principles of the Declaration of Independence. In this module you will evaluate the Declaration of Independence as having emerged from the tradition of American constitutionalism. You will explore the meaning of equality as it is illuminated by this context.

**Learning Outcomes**

Upon successful completion of this module, you will be able to:

- Evaluate the Declaration of Independence against similar declarations issued by colonial and local governments.
- Analyze the structure of the Declaration of Independence and assess the function of the concept of "equality" within that structure.
- Identify consistent themes between the Declaration of Independence and other writings by Thomas Jefferson.

## Week 5 Email from Prof. Root

Wow, time is flying by. If you are getting behind, get caught up and step lively.

**A housekeeping note:** Make sure you review APA manual of style, and always have it on hand as a reference like you would a dictionary, thesaurus, and grammar book.

**Fischer:** What is the proper role for the varying classes in society? You get a sense of the division in this reading. The question is this: can any society function without the division of hierarchy? We need elites to organize, for example, our defense. A plebiscite is still a mob. The generational question that comes from this is: how do we prevent oligarchy from forming out of these ranks? It is next to impossible. Therefore, there is a cycle of regimes, is there not? On the surface it appears like a catch-22. In Virginia we saw the problems with this early on. In the late 1820s–30s we found the Tidewater running the state and keeping slaves among them when those to the west of that region wanted (1) more representation and (2) a ban on the nefarious practice. The old elites won out, and by the Civil War the Appalachian region saw their moment to leave the state — now West Virginia. None of this is in the book, but it is relevant.

**What is American?:** Notice the toleration in the country by the late 1700s. It should strike you that there is no group identity we see from the left today. The author makes the point that this is the strength of the nation. And let me add NATION. A people without a nation are a people ripe for defeat. The character of the early American was disparate and hearty indeed. They may have disagreed, but they believed in an IDEA greater than themselves. This is frankly not so today. We can read this piece as a 1784 representation of being a prepper, as they call it today, or maybe a homesteader. Both of these are alive and growing in the USA today — just search YouTube and you will find a plethora of channels dedicated to the topic. The question as we arrive at the conclusion of the essay is: are there differences between peoples who come here? And what does that do to the original understanding of the people who founded it? How many of us are industrious today? Are you? Do you have the skills to be so? Or are we, as Plato noted, a "city of pigs"? Think on this much.

**Jefferson:** The original Declaration is a remarkable work. Its condemnation against slavery (kept in more moderate language in the final version) is remarkable because it is essentially slave owners stating that slavery was wrong and contrary to the nature of man. Many forget that. Additionally, the original blames the King for keeping slavery in our midst. This is true because Virginia tried to ban it by several means — including onerous taxation. The King voided all those attempts, thus keeping slavery in Old Dominion and allowing it to take root. In the Summary View, we find similar arguments. In it, we find many of the ideas we find in the Declaration two years later. **READ SLOWLY AND CAREFULLY.** I will close this brief section by noting that many of the charges against the King are applicable to our current situation in the present. I would also direct your attention to an annotation of the Declaration here: https://founding.com/the-declaration/founding-principles/

**DeT:** There is equality rightly understood, and then there is equality as understood by the modern world. The latter form is not the former — not even close. Think of it in America today: equality means conditions, but these conditions are leading to a hostility toward other groups — so this is not really equality, is it? Certain races and groups are celebrated, while others are denigrated constantly now, all in the name of "equality." But the word has had violence done to it — as equality today is a badge of racism (they call it equity). DeT notes that this originates from the passions, not reason. He is correct. At the end of the reading, we see that he means that a people will not tolerate an aristocracy, and will clamor for slavery to get it and call it "equality." Yet this phenomenon is not coming from the people per se, as much as it is the ruling class who stir up the passions of the people in order to enslave them. Think on this much, for it seems DeT did not see this inventiveness of evil forming among us.

**Meditation:** Δόξα τῷ Θεῷ πάντων ἕνεκεν — "Glory to God in all things." These are the purported last words of St. John Chrysostom, who is partly responsible for preserving the workings of the inner church. He lived around 300–400 AD. Much of what we know of the literal workings of the early church comes from him (and Basil, which is older). Chrysostom was notable for challenging the political powers of the day for their arrogance and venality. This IS the history of the Church. If you are not familiar, it behooves you to investigate it.
