## I. Executive Summary

Jefferson's draft shows what the Declaration looked like before Congress edited it. The changes are substantial. The draft condemns the British king for blocking colonial attempts to end the slave trade, and it blames George III for the existence of slavery. Congress removed that passage to appease South Carolina and Georgia, and the editor notes that northern delegates also disliked it. For the Week 5 question about equality, the central point is that the preamble's "all men are created equal" and the slavery passage appear in the same document. This shows how the Founders stated equality as a universal principle while leaving slavery in place.

## II. Structure of the Document

1. **Preamble.** Jefferson's text is unchanged except for one substitution. "Self evident" truths include that "all men are created equal," that they are "endowed by their creator with inherent and inalienable rights," and that governments derive "their just powers from the consent of the governed." The right to alter or abolish government is grounded in these truths.
2. **Grievance list.** The king is charged with about twenty specific abuses. Among them are refusing assent to laws, dissolving legislatures, limiting judicial independence, keeping standing armies, cutting off trade, and imposing taxes "without our consent."
3. **The slavery passage.** Jefferson writes that the king has "waged cruel war against human nature itself," "captivating and carrying" people "into slavery," and has "prostituted his negative" to block any attempt to end the slave trade. Jefferson describes the trade as "piratical warfare" and calls it the "opprobrium of INFIDEL powers."
4. **Appeal to Britain and the separation.** Colonists say they have petitioned, warned, and appealed to "British brethren," and that Parliament's claim to authority over them "was no part of our constitution."
5. **Declaration of independence.** The final paragraph declares the colonies "free and independent states." The draft's original language is "reject and renounce all allegiance," while the amended version reads "appealing to the supreme judge of the world."

## III. Key Themes Relevant to Equality

- **Equality as a natural principle.** "All men are created equal" is presented as a self-evident truth, not as a claim about colonial privilege. The Declaration places equality at the root of the right to revolution.
- **Equality versus the slave trade.** The draft's slavery passage makes the connection explicit. The king's crime is treated as violating "the rights of human nature," and the phrase "the LIVES of another" marks the moral claim. Congress deleted this and kept the abstract principle of equality.
- **Equality of the colonists against Parliament.** In the grievance list and the appeal to Britain, the colonists claim equal standing with Britons. Parliament's authority is rejected because it was not part of the colonists' "constitution."
- **Consent of the governed.** Equality supports consent. The argument is that people hold power by right and that governments answer to the people.
- **Human nature and "good people."** The phrase "the good people of these states" makes the people the source of political authority, which supports a broader claim about popular sovereignty.

## IV. Points of Tension for the Week 5 Discussion

- **Universal principle versus practice.** The Declaration asserts universal equality while the draft's own author held enslaved people. The editor's introduction says the draft's condemnation of slavery would have had "a profound impact" on later debate. This is the strongest evidence that the Founders' equality was stated more broadly than it was practiced.
- **Congressional editing.** The removal of the slavery clause shows that equality was negotiated against regional interests. For Week 5, this matters because equality was a political concept, not only a social observation.
- **Tone and audience.** Jefferson's language is harsher than the final text. The editor notes that the less moderate tone was directed partly at American loyalists and at those still undecided.

## V. Connection to Other Week 5 Readings

- **Summary View (1774)** contains many of the same arguments in a longer form, especially the claim that Parliament has no right over the colonies and that the king's veto has blocked efforts to end the slave trade.
- **Crèvecoeur's letter** describes social equality among settlers. Jefferson's draft turns the same idea into a political claim that people are equal by nature.
- **Tocqueville** (Vol. II, Part II) can be used to test whether Jefferson's abstract equality matches the social equality Europeans observed.

## VI. Study Questions

1. Does the original draft convey a different meaning from the signed Declaration? If so, how?
2. Why would Congress remove the slavery passage, and what does that show about the role of equality in the Declaration?
3. How does "all men are created equal" function within the document's larger argument for separation?
4. Does the slavery passage make the king's conduct the cause of slavery, or does it also implicate the colonists? How does the text address this?
5. How does the draft's grievance list connect to the rights later written into the Constitution and the first ten amendments?
