## I. Executive Summary

Brown argues that the Declaration's claim that "all men are created equal" was less a guarantee of equal rights than an aspiration, and that its meaning was unsettled from the start. Before 1774, colonists claimed only the "rights of Englishmen," which excluded most of the population. Between 1772 and 1775 they shifted to natural rights, and the Declaration's universal wording was bold but tactical. Brown traces how the principle was then tested against slavery, religious establishment, property, and sex. Patriots often held slaves while asserting natural equality, and the Virginia and Massachusetts cases show the principle winning in some areas and losing in others. Brown closes by quoting John Jay's "one people" ideal and calls the question of whether it could be fulfilled "a mystery."

## II. The Shift from Rights of Englishmen to Natural Equality (pp. 1–5)

**Thesis.** The natural-equality language was new and radical, but Patriots adopted it without much debate.

**Key mechanisms.**
- *Traditional limits:* the "rights of Englishmen" excluded most of Britain's population. Brown notes that roughly 160,000 eligible voters out of about 9 million people shows how unequal English liberty was (p. 2, citing Jefferson's 1774 *Summary View*).
- *Natural rights turn:* colonists rarely based their protests on natural rights before the 1770s. From 1772 to 1775 they made such claims routine (pp. 3–4).
- *Locke's maxim:* "one Rule of Justice for Rich and Poor" was a cultural ideal, not everyday practice (pp. 3–4).
- *Limited scope:* Patriots sought to purify the British constitution rather than "recast the social order" (p. 4, citing Bailyn).

**Constitutional takeaway.** Equality in 1776 began as equality with other Britons, and only later was it stated as equality of humans.

---

## III. Slavery and the Test of Natural Equality (pp. 7–12)

**Thesis.** The principle condemned slavery in theory, but property rights and racial prejudice blocked action.

**Key mechanisms.**
- *The deleted grievance:* Jefferson's draft blamed George III for the slave trade. Congress deleted it, partly because some colonies still imported slaves (p. 7).
- *Virginia's Bill of Rights:* Mason's "all men are by nature equally free" was written by slaveholders. Brown calls this "aspirational" and notes that Virginia's leaders voted repeatedly to end slave imports (pp. 6–7).
- *Massachusetts:* the legislature did not abolish slavery; the state's highest court did so in the 1780s, reading the 1780 Declaration of Rights as making "all men born free and equal" (pp. 10–12).
- *Hypocrisy versus aspiration:* Brown does not rule out hypocrisy, but argues that critics such as Hutchinson and Johnson erred in dismissing Patriot declarations as empty rhetoric (p. 8).

**Constitutional takeaway.** The Declaration's equality principle became a standard that later courts and reformers used against slavery, even though its authors did not intend that outcome in 1776.

---

## IV. Religion, Race, and Jefferson's Ambivalence (pp. 13–18)

**Thesis.** Jefferson supported natural equality as a principle while holding racial views that undercut it.

**Key mechanisms.**
- *Wythe and Tucker:* in *Hudgins v. Wrights* (1806), Chancellor Wythe applied the Bill of Rights to free a family of pauper children. The Court of Appeals reversed him, and Tucker ruled that race, not the Bill of Rights, decided the case (pp. 13–14).
- *Jefferson on race:* in *Notes on the State of Virginia* (1782), Jefferson ranked blacks as inferior in reason and imagination. Brown quotes his later letters to Banneker and Grégoire, in which he professed to hope the doubts were wrong (pp. 15–17).
- *Religious equality:* Virginia's Statute for Religious Freedom (1786) extended equal treatment of conscience. Massachusetts kept a plural Protestant establishment (pp. 19–22).

**Constitutional takeaway.** The principle of natural equality was applied unevenly, and the boundary between "men" and "outsiders" was disputed for generations.

---

## V. Property, Women, and the Meaning of "All Men" (pp. 23–26)

**Thesis.** Equality in the Revolutionary era meant equal opportunity to acquire property, not equal distribution of it.

**Key mechanisms.**
- *Equal opportunity, not outcome:* Brown cites Gordon Wood's view that framers who spoke of equality in relation to property "meant most obviously equality of opportunity" (p. 24). Jefferson's Newton remark ("lord of the person or property of others") expresses the same idea.
- *Hierarchy alongside equality:* Jefferson's school plan selected "geniuses" from the "rubbish," and Adams spoke of the "common Herd" (p. 24).
- *Women and children:* the framers did not consider natural equality to include them. Brown notes that the Declaration's language could later be read literally to include them (pp. 24–25).

**Constitutional takeaway.** The language of "all men" was used reflexively, not literally, which is why later groups could claim it.

---

## VI. Conclusion: "What Never Was and Never Will Be" (pp. 26–27)

Brown quotes John Jay's 1788 description of Americans as "one people," enjoying "the same national rights, privileges, and protection." Brown calls the realization of that ideal, in a land divided by origin, faith, class, and gender, "a mystery" and "perhaps, what never was and never will be."

## Relevance to the Week 5 Discussion

- **Section 3 (18th century versus modern):** the "equality of opportunity, not distribution" point (p. 24) gives a direct comparison with modern equality of outcome.
- **Replies:** Brown can support a Challenge reply to any claim that the Declaration was simply a statement of fact.
