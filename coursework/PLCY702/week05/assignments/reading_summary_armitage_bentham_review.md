## I. Executive Summary

Bentham, a British legal philosopher, attacked the Declaration line by line in 1776. He argued that its claim to "unalienable" natural rights is self-contradictory, because any government must restrict life, liberty, and happiness in order to secure them. He also argued that the colonists' grievances described routine lawful governance as tyranny. Armitage includes the review to show that in 1776 the Declaration was read abroad mainly as a document of foreign policy and sovereignty under the law of nations, and that its rights preamble was the least persuasive part to critics like Bentham.

---

## II. The Attack on the Preamble and "Unalienable" Rights

**Thesis.** The preamble's doctrine of pre-political natural rights is logically absurd.

**Key mechanisms.**
- *Self-defeating logic:* the Declaration calls life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness unalienable, then admits governments must be instituted to secure them. Bentham replies that every government restricts liberty (imprisonment), interferes with happiness (restraining thieves), and takes life (capital punishment). Establishing any government therefore alienates the rights the Declaration calls unalienable.
- *Legal positivism:* Bentham holds that a right is a legal creation. Where no sovereign law exists, no right exists. Treating moral claims as limits on Parliament makes every government illegitimate and invites anarchy.

**Constitutional takeaway.** This is the sharpest statement of the divide between natural-rights constitutionalism (rights predate the state and bind it) and parliamentary sovereignty (sovereign law creates all rights).

---

## III. Potential Versus Actual Oppression

Bentham argues that the colonists' original complaint was never actual overtaxation or oppression. Their complaint was that they were liable to be taxed and that government might misuse its power. Because the possibility of abuse exists wherever government exists, he says the colonists were rebelling against the basic condition of political authority.

---

## IV. No New Usurpation

Bentham denies that customs enforcement and Admiralty courts were new tyranny. Earlier Parliaments had long exercised the same authority over the colonies, so no new constitutional power was assumed.

---

## V. Reversing Cause and Effect

Bentham answers each of the Declaration's final charges against George III by saying the colonists acted first:

| Charge in the Declaration | Bentham's reply |
|---------------------------|-----------------|
| Cutting off trade | Colonists first tried to cut off British trade through boycotts |
| Seizing colonial vessels | Colonists first burned the King's vessels |
| Sending troops | Colonists first took up arms against royal authority |
| Engaging Indigenous allies | Colonists first recruited them against royal forces |
| Forcing captives onto naval vessels | The King merely spared traitors from the gallows |

---

## VI. Armitage's Global-History Framing

Armitage's central argument is that in 1776 the Declaration was read first as an act of foreign policy, a "declaration of interdependence" seeking recognition from foreign powers. The rights language of the second paragraph only became the presumed heart of American identity decades later, after the War of 1812 and through Abraham Lincoln.

---

## VII. Connections to Fischer and Tocqueville

- **Fischer:** colonial society held several regional liberty traditions that all resisted centralized imperial uniformity.
- **Bentham:** the strongest counterargument from the center of the empire, that pre-political rights are legally incoherent.
- **Tocqueville:** shows how democratic equality can still pull citizens toward a centralized administrative state unless local institutions remain strong.
