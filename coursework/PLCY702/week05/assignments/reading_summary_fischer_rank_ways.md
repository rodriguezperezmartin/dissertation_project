## I. Executive Summary

Fischer argues that the English-speaking colonies did not start with one shared idea of social order or liberty. Each region carried a distinct "folkway" from its British origin, and each folkway produced its own "rank system" and its own conception of liberty. The four sections assigned for Week 5 show that the New England, Chesapeake, Delaware Valley, and related regions were not egalitarian in a modern sense. Each was organized around a particular structure of rank, and that structure shaped how its people understood freedom.

For the Week 5 question on equality, the key point is that colonial "equality" was usually equality within a rank, not equality of condition. The regions differed in whether they compressed or extended the hierarchy and in how they legitimized it.

---

## II. Massachusetts: Truncated Orders (pp. 131–139)

**Thesis.** Puritan New England believed in a divinely ordered hierarchy. Its structure was "truncated": the top (hereditary nobility) and bottom (a large landless class) were largely cut off, leaving a broad middle of yeomen and artisans.

**Key mechanisms.**
- *Visible rank:* titles marked status ("Mr." and "Mrs." for the elite, "Goodman" and "Goodwife" for others), sumptuary norms limited fine dress by rank, and meetinghouse pews were assigned by age, wealth, and standing.
- *Economic compression:* land was distributed through town covenants, not large estates, which made wealth more even than in England or the Chesapeake.
- *Biblical justification:* John Winthrop's reasoning that "some must be rich, some poor" was paired with a communal duty to prevent extreme poverty or excess.

**Constitutional takeaway.** The compressed middle class supported the New England town meeting and high civic participation. This is the pattern Tocqueville later praised in the townships.

---

## III. Virginia: Extended Orders (pp. 279–285)

**Thesis.** Virginia's hierarchy was "extended" at both ends. Under Governor William Berkeley, a self-conscious gentry took the top, and indentured servants and enslaved Africans formed the bottom.

**Key mechanisms.**
- *Steep stratification:* wealth was more unequal than in New England and steeper than in England. A small planter elite controlled land, offices, and vestries.
- *Deference:* rank was reinforced through lineage, honor, hospitality, and ritual respect, with paternalistic duties attached to the gentry.
- *Hegemonic liberty:* in this worldview, liberty meant the autonomy of the ruling class to govern itself and its dependents.

**Constitutional takeaway.** A slaveholding aristocracy could still produce fierce defenders of constitutional independence (Washington, Jefferson, Madison). Their constitutionalism protected local gentry autonomy against distant authority.

---

## IV. William Penn and the Delaware Valley (pp. 330–338)

**Thesis.** Pennsylvania was shaped by William Penn and the Society of Friends. Their "Holy Experiment" aimed at religious toleration, peace, and reciprocal liberty.

**Key mechanisms.**
- *Reciprocal liberty:* Quakers believed in the "Inner Light" in each person. Penn's Frame of Government guaranteed liberty of conscience and fair legal treatment for peaceful settlers, including non-Quakers and Native Americans.
- *Planned pluralism:* Penn recruited English, Welsh, and German groups to build a commercial and peaceful commonwealth.
- *Proprietary tension:* Penn was an aristocratic proprietor who expected quitrents and deference, yet his own theology attracted independent settlers who resisted his authority.

**Constitutional takeaway.** Penn's Frame of Government anticipates later American features: religious liberty, written guarantees of individual rights, and pluralism as public policy.

---

## V. Delaware Rank Ways: Stratification Within a Single Order (pp. 418–421)

**Thesis.** Quakers rejected the English system of legal orders, but the Delaware Valley did not become classless. It developed legal and spiritual equality alongside significant economic inequality.

**Key mechanisms.**
- *Rejection of deference:* Quakers refused "hat honor," rejected titles, and used "thee" and "thou" with everyone.
- *Commercial inequality:* wealthy Philadelphia merchants ("weighty Friends") and poorer artisans and laborers grew apart over time.
- *Class versus order:* status moved from inherited legal rank to achieved economic standing and reputation.

**Constitutional takeaway.** This model anticipates a modern pattern: formal equality under law alongside wide economic stratification produced by markets.

## VI. Additional Section: Backcountry Rank Ways (not on the assigned page list)

The student's notes also cover the Scots-Irish and borderland settlers of the Appalachian backcountry. Their structure combined steep wealth inequality with fierce social egalitarianism. Status had to be earned through courage and personal honor, not inherited deference. Their "natural liberty" was marked by suspicion of distant government and tax collectors. ## VII. Four-Region Comparison

| Region | Rank label | Social structure | Conception of liberty |
|--------|-----------|------------------|-----------------------|
| Massachusetts (Puritans) | Truncated orders | Hierarchy kept, extremes cut off, broad middle | Ordered liberty under moral law |
| Virginia (Cavaliers) | Extended orders | Gentry at top, servants and enslaved at bottom | Hegemonic liberty for the elite |
| Delaware Valley (Quakers) | Single order, stratified | No formal ranks, but commercial inequality | Reciprocal liberty and equal legal rights |
| Backcountry (Scots-Irish) | Personal rank | Wealth gap, but no deference | Natural liberty and clan autonomy |
