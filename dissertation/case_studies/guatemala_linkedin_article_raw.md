Digital Transformation of Health in Guatemala: A Path Toward Universal Health Coverage
Daniel Otzoy - Garcia
Daniel Otzoy - Garcia
Digital Transformation | Strategy | AI | Project Management | Public Sector & Health | Sharing lessons from the field


October 31, 2025
Across the world, digital health has become a strategic enabler of Universal Health Coverage (UHC) — ensuring that everyone, everywhere, can access quality health services without financial hardship. The World Health Organization’s Global Strategy on Digital Health (2020–2025) sets a clear direction: countries must leverage digital innovation to build more equitable, efficient, and resilient health systems.

Yet, the journey toward digital maturity is uneven. By 2024, almost half of the countries assessed globally remained at intermediate levels of digital health development, and only 4% had reached the highest maturity stage. For many low- and middle-income countries, the digital foundation is still under construction.

Regional perspective: a shared digital health vision
In Latin America and the Caribbean (LAC), governments are increasingly embracing this transformation. Through the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO), the region adopted a Digital Health Transformation Roadmap, a Plan of Action on Health Information Systems, and Eight Principles for Digital Transformation aligned with the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development.

These principles go beyond technology. They emphasize universal connectivity, digital inclusion for vulnerable populations, interoperability, human rights-based governance, and the responsible use of emerging technologies such as artificial intelligence. They also call for robust institutional architectures and cybersecurity as foundations of trust.

In essence, the digitalization of health in LAC is not merely a technical agenda — it’s a social and political one. It’s about rethinking how governments, private sector, and citizens collaborate to ensure that no one is left behind in accessing healthcare.

Guatemala’s journey toward digital transformation
In this regional context, Guatemala has made important strides to modernize its health sector through digital tools and data-driven approaches. In December 2022, the country launched its National Digital Health Strategy 2022–2032, establishing a 10-year vision for integrating technology into health services.

This strategy aligns with international principles and prioritizes the interoperability of information systems, the strengthening of data governance, and the digital literacy of health workers. It also highlights the importance of updating regulations and ensuring cybersecurity in all health networks.

A significant milestone came in 2023, when Guatemala held its Second International Workshop on Digital Health Interoperability, a key step toward building a National Interoperability Framework. This framework seeks to connect disparate systems — from epidemiological surveillance to electronic medical records and telemedicine — allowing data to flow securely across the health sector.

From policy to practice: real-world impact
Digital transformation is not just happening on paper. Guatemala already has tangible experiences showing how technology can bring healthcare closer to people who need it most.

In 2020, with the support of PAHO, the European Union, and Sweden, the Ministry of Health launched the first real-time telemedicine networks in remote areas of Quiché and San Marcos. Equipped with satellite connectivity and digital diagnostic tools, these tele-clinics have enabled specialized consultations for rural communities that previously had little or no access to healthcare.

Such initiatives directly contribute to reducing maternal mortality and child malnutrition — two of the country’s most pressing challenges. They also embody the core idea of universal health coverage: equity in access, regardless of geography or socioeconomic status.

Moreover, Guatemala’s digital health agenda has gained momentum thanks to strong international partnerships. The country works closely with organizations such as the Inter-American Development Bank (IDB), USAID, and the United Nations to strengthen institutional capacity and develop sustainable digital ecosystems.

One of the most promising initiatives is the “Wayfree” public-private alliance, supported by the European Union and BID Lab, which aims to provide free internet access in 100% of Guatemalan municipalities. This unprecedented effort to close the digital divide will have transformative effects not only in education and commerce but also in health — ensuring that connectivity becomes a public good and not a privilege.

Challenges that remain
Despite the progress, Guatemala faces persistent gaps in its digital transformation journey. Many health facilities still lack adequate infrastructure and stable internet connectivity. Health information systems remain fragmented, making it difficult to obtain an integrated view of patients and services.

Equally important is the human factor: successful digital health depends on people who are trained, motivated, and supported to adopt new technologies. Strengthening digital skills among health professionals and promoting digital literacy in the population are essential to ensure that innovation translates into real benefits.

Regulatory and governance frameworks also need to evolve. While the National Strategy provides a solid foundation, its effective implementation will require updated legislation, sustainable financing, and inter-institutional coordination. Above all, political continuity is critical — digital transformation must become a long-term State policy, not just a government initiative.

Turning gaps into opportunities
Identifying these gaps also reveals clear opportunities for action.

Connectivity and equipment: Expanding universal internet access and investing in reliable digital infrastructure should be national priorities.
Interoperability: Implementing international standards like HL7 FHIR can enable safe, efficient data exchange across systems.
Capacity building: Continuous training programs in digital health and change management can help overcome resistance and build confidence among healthcare workers.
Legal modernization: Updating laws on health data protection, teleconsultation, and digital signatures will foster trust and safeguard citizens’ rights.
Inclusion: Every digital health initiative must be designed through the lens of equity — addressing the needs of indigenous, rural, elderly, and disabled populations.

A vision for the future
Guatemala stands at a decisive moment. The foundations of digital health are in place, and the momentum is growing. The next step is to consolidate leadership, ensure governance, and sustain investment.

The ultimate goal is not to digitize for the sake of technology, but to transform healthcare delivery — making it more inclusive, data-driven, and responsive to people’s real needs.

As I often emphasize, what transforms is not technology itself, but health through technology. When digital tools are guided by a clear vision, strong institutions, and a commitment to human well-being, they can become powerful equalizers of opportunity.

Guatemala has the potential to lead by example in Latin America: demonstrating how digital innovation, guided by values of equity and resilience, can help achieve Universal Health Coverage and improve lives for all.
