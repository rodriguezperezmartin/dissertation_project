# Theoretical Framework: DPI-Fostering Policy Instruments on Stone's Carrot/Stick Spectrum

**Purpose:** Apply Deborah Stone's (2012) *Policy Paradox* Chapter 11 (Inducements) framework — already fully summarized in [coursework/PLCY700/week05/reading_notes.md](../../coursework/PLCY700/week05/reading_notes.md) — to classify the real-world policy instruments governments actually use to foster digital public infrastructure (DPI) in healthcare, using the Mexico and Guatemala case studies as grounding evidence.

## Core Premise
Governments do not build healthcare technology themselves — clinicians, hospitals, and private-sector vendors do. What governments *do* have is the power to wield **inducements** (Stone, Ch. 11): positive rewards ("carrots") and negative sanctions ("sticks") that change the cost-benefit calculus facing providers, institutions, and vendors. This reframes the dissertation's central question from a purely technical one ("does AI/interoperability reduce reactive spending?") to a **policy-instrument-design question**: which inducement — and under what capability conditions — actually produces the shift from reactive to proactive health spending?

## Classifying Real-World DPI Instruments on Stone's Spectrum

| Instrument | Country/Example | Stone Classification | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| General Health Law reform (Jan 2026) — paper records made illegal | Mexico | **Negative inducement / sanction (stick)** | Legal prohibition — non-compliance is now unlawful, not merely discouraged |
| NOM-024-SSA3-2012 certification requirement | Mexico | **Sanction via standard-setting (stick, procedural)** | Non-certified systems cannot legally operate as interoperable/secure — compliance gatekeeping rather than a direct behavioral command |
| National Digital Health Strategy 2022–2032 | Guatemala | **Non-coercive roadmap / voluntary coordination (neither carrot nor stick)** | No legal penalty for non-adoption; functions as a planning and coordination instrument, reliant on donor partnership and institutional buy-in rather than compulsion |
| Wayfree public-private alliance (free internet in 100% of municipalities) | Guatemala | **Positive inducement (carrot) — subsidized resource provision** | Removes a cost barrier (connectivity) rather than penalizing its absence |
| PAHO/EU/Sweden-funded telemedicine pilots (Quiché, San Marcos) | Guatemala | **Positive inducement (carrot) — donor-funded capability grant** | Provides capability (satellite connectivity, diagnostic tools) directly, rather than mandating providers find it themselves |
| U.S. agricultural crop subsidies | United States (user's own comparative example) | **Positive inducement (carrot) — classic economic subsidy** | Rewards a specific behavior (growing designated crops) with direct payment, the textbook Stone "carrot" |
| Mandatory electronic invoicing (2014) | Mexico (analogy cited by industry) | **Sanction (stick)** — precedent instrument | Cited by Mexican HealthTech Association as evidence that *mandating* (not just encouraging) a digital practice can trigger rapid sectoral adoption — a direct precedent for testing whether the same stick-based logic works for health records |

## Applying Stone's "Core Operational Prerequisites for Inducement Success"
From the Week 5 reading notes (Stone, 2012, pp. 274–277), an inducement only succeeds if **all four** conditions hold simultaneously: (1) Awareness, (2) Value Alignment/Valence, (3) Capability/Resource Control, (4) Credibility of Enforcement. Scoring the two cases:

| Prerequisite | Mexico (mandate/stick) | Guatemala (roadmap + donor carrots) |
|---|---|---|
| **Awareness** | High — nationally published decree, widely reported | Moderate — strategy exists but is less legally binding/visible to providers |
| **Value Alignment** | Mixed — providers may value compliance to avoid penalty, but also report using duplicate paper+digital workflows due to distrust/complexity | Likely higher where donor-funded pilots directly demonstrate value (maternal mortality/malnutrition reduction) |
| **Capability** | **Low — the core failure point.** Public health spending (2.7% GDP) far below OECD average (7.6%); connectivity gaps; NOM-024 non-compliance; undisclosed pact budget | Donor-funded capability investment present (Wayfree connectivity, telemedicine equipment) but still described as insufficient/fragmented overall |
| **Credibility of Enforcement** | High — paper records are now explicitly illegal (strong legal "stick") | Low — no legal penalty structure identified; relies on continuity of political will and donor funding |

**Testable implication for the dissertation:** Stone's framework predicts that **Mexico's mandate will underperform its legal strength** because it fails the Capability prerequisite, while **Guatemala's weaker legal mandate may outperform expectations in pilot regions** specifically because donor funding satisfies the Capability prerequisite that Mexico's mandate lacks. This is a concrete, falsifiable comparative hypothesis the dissertation could test against future outcome data from both countries.

## Refined Theoretical Contribution
This framework suggests a refinement to the dissertation's core thesis: it is not enough to ask whether DPI reduces reactive crisis spending — the **policy instrument type matters, and its success is conditional on whether Capability (funding, connectivity, training, standards) is provided alongside legal/regulatory force.** A mandate without capability investment (Mexico's apparent current trajectory) risks becoming a "false balance" in the Proverbs 11:1 sense already invoked in the Week 2 short paper — a rule without the resources to make it true. A roadmap with strong donor-funded capability investment but weak legal force (Guatemala) risks political discontinuity once donor funding or administrations change. The dissertation's contribution could be to identify the **combination** of instrument type + capability investment + enforcement credibility most predictive of an actual reactive-to-proactive spending shift.
